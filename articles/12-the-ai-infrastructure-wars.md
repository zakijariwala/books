# Headline options
1. The cloud promised infinite. The AI era found the bottom.
2. Money was never the bottleneck. A chip was, and a power line.
3. Everyone treats compute like electricity from a wall socket. In 2024 the wall pushed back.

---

The team had the model, the money, and the plan. What they did not have, and could not get, was the machines.

This was 2024. They had raised a round on the strength of something that genuinely worked, and they walked into the meeting expecting the easy part. You buy the computers. You have always been able to buy the computers. For fifteen years the entire promise of the cloud was that you could summon as many as you wanted, this afternoon, and give them back tonight. Instead they got a date. Months out. For the specific kind of machine they needed, the answer was not a price. It was a waitlist, and the waitlist did not care how much they had raised.

That was the moment a lot of smart people learned something the industry had spent a decade helping them forget. Compute is not infinite. It never was. It only looked infinite because, for a long time, demand stayed comfortably below what the warehouses could hold. The AI era ended that, and it ended it fast, and watching the biggest and richest companies in the world scramble in response was the most honest look at how this all actually works that we have had in years.

Here is the idea, and it is older than any chip. Demand can move in months. The things that satisfy demand move in years. That gap is the whole story.

Think about electricity, because the cloud has always wanted you to. Flip a switch and the light comes on, and you never once wonder whether there is enough. It feels infinite, and it feels instant, and it is neither. It is a power plant somebody built years ago, sized for a peak somebody guessed at, connected by lines that took a decade to permit. On an ordinary day you never touch the ceiling, so you forget the ceiling exists. Then a heat wave arrives, every air conditioner in the region turns on at once, and the grid that felt infinite reveals its bottom in an afternoon. Adding more does not take an afternoon. It takes years.

Artificial intelligence was the heat wave.

The scarce thing was never money, and this is the part that surprises business people, because money is the thing they are used to being scarce. In the AI build-out, money was abundant. What was scarce was a particular chip, made in meaningful volume by essentially one company, using manufacturing capacity that itself takes years to expand. And beneath the chip, scarcer still, was power. A warehouse full of these machines draws electricity like a small town. You cannot just plug in a small town. You need a substation, and a line to the grid, and a grid with room to spare, and by 2024 the companies building these places were not shopping for real estate so much as shopping for megawatts, in places where the power actually existed.

So the giants did what you do when the bottleneck is physical. They stopped competing on features and started competing on concrete and copper. Through 2023, 2024, and into 2025, the race quietly became a race to secure chips before anyone else, to lock up power years in advance, and to pour buildings at a pace that the industry had never attempted. Regions filled up. The dropdown that used to offer you any location you liked started returning the digital equivalent of "no vacancy" for the machines everyone suddenly wanted. There was a pecking order, and where you sat on it decided whether your plan happened this quarter or next year.

The pecking order ran on more than politeness. By 2024 a good deal of the money being raised across the industry was raised, in effect, to buy a place in line, prepaying for capacity that would not arrive for months and signing multi-year commitments for machines that did not exist yet. Established companies with long relationships and deep pockets got served first. Everyone else took a number. The clouds themselves, the landlords who had spent a decade insisting the supply was endless, were now the ones rationing it, because they had run into the same wall as their tenants and hit it harder.

This is the misconception, and it is the most expensive one in this whole field, because it hides inside a phrase everyone says without thinking. "We'll just scale up when we need to." People say it in planning meetings the way they say the sun will rise. It treats capacity as weather, as something that is simply there when you turn to look. And for years, for most workloads, it was close enough to true that saying it cost nothing. The AI era is the proof that it was never actually true. It was a loan against a ceiling nobody was hitting, and the bill came due the moment enough people reached for the same scarce thing at the same time.

The vendors will not lead with this. Their brochures still say elastic and infinite and on-demand, because those words sell, and because for the ordinary workload they remain mostly honest. But strip the language back and the truth underneath is physical and slow. Somewhere there is a fab that took years to build. Somewhere there is a power plant. Somewhere there is a field with cranes in it and a substation going up next door, and that field is the real reason your plan either happens on time or does not. The cloud did not abolish the physical world. It rented it to you by the hour and hoped you would not ask where it was.

Now the part you can use, and it does not require you to know a thing about the chips. In any plan that leans on the word "just," as in "we'll just add more when the time comes," find the physical bottleneck and ask two questions about it. What is the one resource this depends on that cannot be bought instantly? And how long does it actually take to add more of that resource? Sometimes the answer is comfortable and you move on. Sometimes the answer is "one company makes it and the lead time is measured in quarters," and you have just found the assumption that will break your roadmap, while there is still time to plan around it instead of discovering it on a waitlist.

The team from the beginning survived, in the end. They rearranged the plan around the calendar the machines gave them instead of the calendar they had given themselves. It cost them half a year and taught them, permanently, to treat capacity as a supply chain rather than a switch. That is a cheap lesson compared to the ones learned by the companies that assumed the switch would always work.

So here is my question, and you can answer it about your own most confident plan. Where in it are you assuming that something will simply be available when you reach for it? And have you ever once checked how long it takes to make more?
