# Case study: media company adopting generative AI

## The scenario

**Wexley Broadcasting** produces and licenses factual programming: documentary
series, archive footage, and a nightly news operation. It sells finished
programmes to distributors and licenses individual clips from an archive of
about a hundred and twenty thousand hours going back sixty years.

Wexley migrated to Google Cloud three years ago and the migration is done. This
is the distinguishing feature of the scenario, and the shape of the questions
follows from it: nothing here is a lift and shift, and every answer about moving
a workload to the cloud is answering a question nobody asked.

The estate today: media files in Cloud Storage across several buckets with
inconsistent lifecycle rules; a GKE Standard cluster running the editorial
tools, the licensing portal, and the internal search application; Cloud
Functions handling ingest events when new material lands; a BigQuery warehouse
holding licensing transactions, viewing figures, and playout logs; monitoring
split between Cloud Monitoring and a third-party tool the operations team
prefers. Two edit suites still run on-premises with a Kubernetes cluster the
post-production team maintains, because the finishing software is licensed to
local hardware.

The archive's problem is metadata. Material catalogued before 2004 has a title,
a date, and a paragraph typed by a librarian. Searching it means knowing what
you are looking for already. Licensing revenue is limited by what customers can
find.

Stated requirements:

- Generate descriptive metadata — subjects, locations, spoken content, on-screen
  text — automatically across the archive, and keep it current as new material
  arrives.
- Customers must be able to search the archive in natural language, and results
  must cite the specific clip and timecode. Fabricated results are a commercial
  and legal problem.
- The licensing portal must meet a 99.9% availability objective. It currently
  runs at about 99.5% and nobody can say why.
- The two on-premises clusters must come under the same policy and configuration
  management as the cloud cluster. Post-production will not give up local
  hardware.
- Deployments are manual, run by three people, with no consistent approval step.
- Generative processing must not exceed an agreed monthly budget, and the
  finance director wants attribution by department.

## Working the problem

**Metadata generation is mostly not a generative problem.** Speech
transcription, on-screen text extraction, object and scene labelling are
standard tasks with prebuilt APIs. Train nothing for them. The generative layer
sits above: summarising a clip into a description, inferring subject matter from
transcript plus labels, normalising sixty years of inconsistent terminology.
The exam's most common error is reaching for custom training when a managed API
meets the requirement, and this scenario is built to expose it.

Run the backfill as batch, not as a live endpoint. A hundred and twenty thousand hours
processed once is a job, not a service, and an always-on prediction endpoint for
it is a cost error. New material arriving is the streaming case, and the
existing Cloud Functions ingest path already carries the trigger.

**Natural-language search with citations is retrieval, not fine-tuning.** The
requirement that results cite a specific clip and timecode settles it: a
fine-tuned model produces plausible text and cannot attribute. Embed the
generated metadata and transcript segments, store the vectors, retrieve on
query, and pass the retrieved segments to the model as grounding context. The
citation comes from the retrieved chunk, which is a record in your own system.
Where the vectors live is a genuine choice — a managed vector search service, or
vector support inside a database or warehouse already in the estate. Prefer what
the team already runs unless the query volume justifies otherwise.

**The availability gap.** Nobody can say why the portal runs at 99.5%, and that
is the finding. Before adding redundancy, define an indicator that reflects what
customers experience — successful licensing requests served under a latency
threshold — and instrument it. Then look for the causes: a zonal cluster whose
control plane outages stop rollouts and autoscaling, health checks that verify a
port rather than the application, no error budget so releases never pause. The
correct first move is measurement and root cause analysis, not a second region.
Multi-region here is a distractor priced at multi-region rates.

**The hybrid clusters.** Two on-premises clusters plus one in the cloud, under
one policy plane, is the fleet requirement. Register all three to a fleet and
apply configuration and policy from a Git repository, so a control asserted once
lands everywhere and drift is corrected rather than reported. The post-production
hardware constraint is permanent, so this is the steady state, not a transition.

**Delivery.** Manual deployments by three people with no approval step is both
a reliability finding and an audit one. Cloud Build for the pipeline, Artifact
Registry for images, Cloud Deploy to carry promotion with an approval gate, and
Binary Authorization to enforce that only images built by that pipeline run in
production.

**Cost.** Inference cost control means batching the backfill, caching repeated
queries, and choosing a smaller model where it suffices — the largest model is
rarely the correct answer to a budgeted requirement. Attribution by department
comes from labels and project structure, not from a reporting tool, and the
budget alert notifies rather than caps. If finance wants spend to stop, the
alert must be wired to an action.

## The tradeoff you defend

The attractive answer is to fine-tune a foundation model on the archive so it
"knows" the collection. It demonstrates ambition, it produces fluent
descriptions, and it is wrong.

Fine-tuning teaches behaviour and format. It does not create attributable
knowledge, and it goes stale the moment new material arrives. The requirement
says results must cite clip and timecode, and fabricated results carry legal
consequence. Retrieval keeps the source of truth in a system Wexley controls,
makes every answer traceable to a record, and updates when the archive does.
Tuning may still earn a place later, for tone and description format — but as
the smaller half of the design, not the foundation of it.

The second tradeoff is the availability one. Adding a region would improve the
number and would not explain it. Spending on redundancy before measurement is
how organisations buy nines they cannot demonstrate.

## What the exam would ask

Metadata must be generated across a hundred and twenty thousand hours of archive and
kept current as new material arrives. Which processing modes, and what
distinguishes them?

Search results must cite the source clip. Which pattern does that requirement
select, and which does it eliminate?

The licensing portal misses its objective and nobody knows why. What is the
first architectural action, and why is a second region wrong here?

Post-production keeps two on-premises clusters permanently. How do all three
clusters receive the same policy?
