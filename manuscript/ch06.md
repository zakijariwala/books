# Chapter 6: Networking, Roads and Bouncers

The customer in Singapore said the product was slow. The team in London opened it, watched it respond in a blink, and closed the ticket.

This happened three times over five months. Each time the account manager relayed the complaint, each time an engineer checked and found nothing, and each time the ticket was closed with a note saying performance appeared normal. The engineers were not being lazy. They opened the product, it was fast, and there was nothing on any of their charts to suggest otherwise. Every measurement they had said the system was healthy.

The fourth time, the customer's operations director joined a call and shared her screen. The room watched a page take four seconds to appear that took under one in London. Nobody had been imagining anything. The product was fast and slow at the same time, depending on where you were standing.

What none of the engineers had accounted for is that they were sitting a short distance from the machines. The customer was sitting eleven thousand kilometres from them. That distance is not a metaphor for anything. It is a physical fact with a fixed price in time, it applies to every single request the product makes, and no amount of adding capacity at the London end changes it by a millisecond.

## The business problem

Distance costs time, and time costs money in ways that arrive on different desks.

It arrives in sales, where a prospect trials the product from a country you do not serve well and forms an impression in the first ninety seconds. Nobody in that trial fills in a form explaining that the application felt sluggish. They stop replying to emails, and the loss is recorded as no decision.

It arrives in support, as tickets that cannot be reproduced. Those tickets are corrosive beyond their number, because a customer who has reported a real problem three times and been told it does not exist stops reporting problems and starts shopping.

It arrives in expansion plans, where somebody proposes selling into a new region and the question of whether the product will be usable there gets answered with a shrug, or worse, with confidence.

And it arrives in the bill, because every remedy for distance costs something, and the remedies are not interchangeable.

There is a compounding version of this that decides where companies can grow. A business whose product is usable in three countries and sluggish in forty has quietly chosen its market without holding a meeting about it. Sales will concentrate where the product performs, because that is where deals close, and the territory map will come to match the infrastructure map without anyone drawing the connection. Distance is therefore not only a support problem. It is a constraint on strategy, applied silently, by physics.

What a business reader needs from this chapter is the ability to say, without hedging, why the distant customer is slower, and to know the three ways of fixing it and what each one costs. That is a complete answer to a question most companies handle by closing the ticket.

## The roads

Two different things get called speed, and confusing them is the source of most bad decisions in this area.

**Bandwidth is how wide the road is.** It governs how much can travel at once. A wider road moves more lorries per hour, which matters when you are shipping something large, such as the wedding photograph from chapter two.

**Latency is how long the trip takes.** It governs how long any single journey lasts, no matter how small the cargo. A postcard and a pallet leaving London for Singapore arrive at roughly the same time, because the limit is the distance, not the size.

Widening a road does not shorten it. This is the sentence to carry out of the chapter, because the standard response to "the product is slow in Singapore" is to buy more capacity, and capacity is width. The customer's problem was length.

The length has a floor set by physics. Information travels through glass fibre at a large but finite speed, and the cables do not run in straight lines; they follow coastlines, seabeds, and political boundaries. London to Singapore and back is a measurable number of milliseconds that nobody can negotiate down, and every engineer who has tried has discovered they are arguing with the speed of light.

The word "network" hides how physical this is. The connection between two continents is a cable, lying on a seabed, laid by a ship, owned by a consortium, and vulnerable to anchors and earthquakes. When one of them is cut, traffic takes a longer route and everything served over it gets slower for weeks, and a company in one country discovers that its customers in another have degraded for reasons no one on the payroll can influence. There is a map of these cables. It looks like a shipping chart, because it is one, and it is a better picture of the internet than any diagram with a cloud on it.

The company in this chapter also had no way of knowing which route its customer's traffic took. Two customers the same distance away can have different experiences because one sits behind a well-connected network and one does not. The distance sets the floor; the route sets what you get above the floor, and the route belongs to companies you have no contract with.

If that were the whole story, the Singapore customer would have waited a fraction of a second longer and nobody would have noticed. Here is why she waited four seconds instead.

**A single page is not one journey. It is many.** The browser asks for the page, which tells it to ask for the layout, which tells it to ask for a set of resources, and along the way it asks who the user is, whether they are permitted, what their settings are, and what data goes in the table. Each of those is a separate trip down the road and back. Twenty round trips is unremarkable for an ordinary page. Some are worse.

Twenty round trips at a short distance is a blink. Twenty round trips across the planet is the four seconds the operations director demonstrated on that call.

The wedding photograph from chapter two showed the same arithmetic in miniature. The friend at the wedding saw it about half a second before the sister three cities away, and that gap was distance doing its work on a handful of journeys. Stretch the distance from three cities to eleven thousand kilometres, raise the journeys from a handful to twenty, and the half second becomes the four seconds that lost a company its credibility with a customer.

There is a corollary that catches teams out when they try to fix this. Making each individual journey faster is difficult and buys little. Removing journeys is easier and buys a great deal. A team that shaves a few milliseconds off the machine's response time has improved the wrong number by a small amount; a team that combines twelve requests into three has improved the right number by a lot. The instinct runs the wrong way because the machine's response time is the number on the dashboard, and the number of journeys is on no dashboard at all.

So the arithmetic that explains the whole complaint is this: **the number of round trips multiplied by the length of the road.** A product that makes many small requests is a product that punishes distance, and it will feel fine to everybody sitting near the machines and terrible to everybody else, which is exactly the pattern that makes a ticket unreproducible.

## The bouncers

The road has doors on it, and somebody decides who gets through each one.

Between the customer and the machine sits a series of checkpoints. Some decide whether traffic is allowed to reach a particular machine at all. Some decide whether this user is who they claim to be. Some are watching for attacks and turning away anything that looks like one. Some sit at the boundary between two parts of your own system, because a well-arranged company does not let every part of its own software talk to every other part without controls.

They exist for good reasons and they are the subject of chapter ten. They matter here for two practical reasons.

The first is that each checkpoint takes a little time, and checkpoints tend to accumulate. Nobody ever removes one, because removing a control is a decision somebody would have to own, which is the same pattern as the undeleted data in chapter five.

The second is that when something is unreachable rather than slow, a bouncer is usually the reason, and this is worth knowing because the symptom is confusing. A customer reports that a feature works for their colleague and not for them. Both are using the same product. The difference is that one is on the office network and one is at home, and a checkpoint somewhere has an opinion about that which nobody documented. Problems that depend on where the user is sitting, rather than who they are, are usually a door rather than a fault.

That distinction is worth carrying into any conversation about an outage, because it sorts the possibilities fast. If a thing is slow for everyone, look at the machine. If it is slow for some places and not others, look at the road. If it works for some people and fails outright for others in a pattern that follows location rather than account, look at the doors. Three symptoms, three different teams, and a company that cannot tell them apart will send every report to whichever team is easiest to find.

The corporate customers make this harder in a way worth anticipating. A large client runs its own doors, and their security team has opinions about what their staff may reach. When your product stops working for one client and no one else, the checkpoint that changed may not be yours at all. That conversation goes better if somebody on your side already knows the difference between a road problem and a door problem, because the alternative is two companies each investigating their own half and finding nothing.

## The three ways to fix distance

There are three, they are different in cost and in kind, and knowing which one is being proposed is most of what a business reader needs.

**One: move a copy of the product closer.** Run the whole thing in a region near the customer, as chapter three described. This works, and it is the heaviest option available. You now operate the product in two places, which means two of everything that can break, two sets of changes to keep in step, and the problem of what happens when a customer's data must exist in one place and their colleague is in the other. It also raises the residency question from chapter three, sometimes helpfully, since a copy in the customer's country may be exactly what their regulator wanted.

**Two: put the heavy, unchanging things closer.** Most of what a page loads is identical for every user: images, layout, code, the preview from chapter two. Copies of those can be kept in many cities at once, so the customer collects them locally rather than from London. The industry has a name for this arrangement and it is not worth learning; what matters is the trade. It is far cheaper than option one, it is well understood, and it helps only with the parts that are the same for everybody. The customer's own data still comes from wherever it lives.

**Three: make fewer round trips.** This is the option nobody proposes in the meeting, and it is often the largest win available. If the page makes twenty journeys and could make five, the distant customer's wait falls by three quarters and nothing needs to be built in another country. This is work inside the product rather than a purchase, which is why it does not come up when the conversation is framed as an infrastructure problem.

The order in which companies consider these is usually the reverse of the order that serves them. Option one is the most visible and gets proposed first because it sounds decisive. Option three is invisible, unglamorous, and frequently the answer.

The reason for that inversion is organisational rather than technical. Option one is a budget request, which is a thing a manager knows how to approve. Option three is a piece of engineering work with no obvious owner, competing against features on a roadmap, benefiting customers who are not in the room. Approving a second region feels like taking the complaint seriously. Rewriting a page to make five requests instead of twenty feels like housekeeping. The first is expensive and visible, the second is cheap and invisible, and organisations reward visibility.

It is worth adding what none of the three options can do. If a customer's own data lives in London and the customer is in Singapore, somebody has to carry that data across the planet the first time they ask for it. Copies in many cities do not help, because the data is unique to them and changes. The only remedies are moving their data closer, which is option one with all its weight, or asking for it fewer times, which is option three again. There is no arrangement in which a distant customer reads their own live data at local speed, and any vendor implying otherwise is describing a cache and hoping you do not ask what happens when the underlying record changes.

## What Meera does with this

Meera now has a sentence for the ticket that keeps getting closed. **"It is not slow for us because we are next to it. Distance is a fixed cost per round trip, and we make a lot of round trips."**

That reframes the conversation from a dispute about whether the problem is real into a question about how many trips the product makes, which somebody can measure this week.

Her three questions, in order:

**"How far is that customer from where we run?"** Not which vendor, not which zone. The physical distance. This is the number that sets the floor, and it is knowable in an afternoon.

**"How many round trips does this page make?"** This is the multiplier, and it is the number the company controls. A team that has never measured it will be surprised by the answer, and the surprise is usually the finding.

**"Which of the three fixes are we proposing, and did we consider the other two?"** If somebody has proposed opening a second region, this question is worth asking before the budget is committed, because option two is much cheaper and option three may be free.

There is a fourth use, and it belongs at the start of plans rather than the end. When the company decides to sell into a distant market, Meera can ask what the experience will be like from there before anyone signs a customer. That question costs nothing at the planning stage and a great deal when asked by a customer's operations director on a call five months in.

And there is a discipline she can apply to her own roadmap. Features that add round trips are cheap to build and expensive at distance. A team can add three small requests to a page in an afternoon, and every one of those is another journey for every distant user forever. Nobody notices in the office. Everyone notices in Singapore.

This is the same shape as the storage ratchet from the last chapter, and it is worth noticing that the shape repeats. Small decisions, each defensible, each invisible on its own, accumulating in one direction with no natural moment of review. Data that is never deleted. Checkpoints that are never removed. Round trips that are never consolidated. In every case the individual addition is trivial and the aggregate is the thing customers complain about, and in every case the fix requires somebody senior enough to schedule unglamorous work.

The most useful thing Meera can do with all of this may be smaller than any of the three fixes. She can ask, once, that somebody measure the product from where the customers are rather than from where the team is. That measurement costs an afternoon, it turns an unresolvable dispute into a number, and until it exists every conversation about distant performance is two groups of reasonable people describing different products to each other.

## Inside a real organization

Every engineering team in the world is sitting close to its own machines, and this is the structural reason distant customers get disbelieved.

The people who build the product, the people who test it, and the people who watch the dashboards are all near the servers, either physically or by working from an office whose network takes a good route. Their experience of the product is the best experience available, and it is the one they defend in tickets. The tooling makes it worse rather than better, because charts measure how long the machine took to answer, which was a few milliseconds and looks healthy, and cannot see the seconds the customer spent waiting for twenty journeys to complete.

The result is a company that has measured the wrong half of the problem and concluded there is no problem.

The second pattern is the one that shows up on the bill, and it connects to chapter five. Moving data between two regions is outbound movement, which is the metered direction. A company that opens a second region to serve distant customers has, without discussing it, committed to a permanent flow of data between the two, and that flow is a line on the invoice that grows with the business. The decision was framed as a performance improvement. It arrives in finance as a data transfer cost, and the two conversations happen in different rooms, months apart, with no one connecting them.

The third pattern is that distance problems get discovered by sales rather than by engineering. The first credible signal is usually a prospect in a new market going quiet, and by the time anyone traces that back to a slow trial, the deal has been lost and recorded as a competitive loss rather than a technical one.

This is worth dwelling on, because it means the data that would prove the problem exists is held by the people least able to interpret it and the loss is filed under the wrong cause. An account executive knows that deals in one territory close at half the rate of deals elsewhere. An engineer knows the servers respond in a few milliseconds. Neither has any reason to walk down the corridor, and the correlation between those two facts is invisible to both. Somebody has to be looking at the company rather than at a department, which is the argument for a product manager owning this question and not a technical team.

The fourth pattern is the one that survives the fix. Once a company opens a second region and the distant customers get faster, the organisation concludes the problem is solved and stops thinking about distance. But the round-trip count keeps rising, because features keep getting added, and the second region only bought back the distance term. Three years later the same complaint returns from the same territory, and this time buying another region will not help, because the multiplier grew rather than the distance. The fix that worked once trains everybody to reach for it again.

## The misconception

The belief is that the internet is instant, and that slowness is therefore evidence of something being wrong.

It is not instant. It is fast, which is a different property. Every request is a physical journey with a duration set by how far apart two things are, and no one is going to fix that, because it is not broken. A product that feels instant nearby and sluggish far away is not malfunctioning. It is behaving exactly as the physics requires, and the only question is whether anyone designed for it.

The costly half of this belief is the corollary: that slow means the system needs more capacity. Capacity is width, distance is length, and buying more width to solve a length problem is a purchase that changes nothing while looking decisive. Companies do this. The bill goes up, the Singapore customer is unaffected, and the failure to improve is taken as evidence that the problem was never real.

For a reader with no company in this, the same correction explains something everyone has experienced. A video call with someone on another continent has a small delay that nobody can engineer away, and it is the reason those conversations develop a rhythm of interruption and apology. It is not the connection being poor. It is the planet being large, and every one of those conversations is a demonstration that information has a speed limit and you can feel it.

It also explains why some things that ought to be equivalent are not. A film streams from a distant country without trouble, because it arrived in advance and is being read from a copy near you, which is option two doing its work. A live sports broadcast from the same country cannot do that, and arrives a few seconds behind the stadium, which is why a neighbour's shout can spoil a goal before your screen shows it. Same distance, different arrangement, and the difference is whether the thing could be sent ahead. Every architecture decision in this chapter is a version of that question.

## Summary

Three things to keep.

Bandwidth is how wide the road is, latency is how long the trip takes, and widening a road does not shorten it. Most complaints about distant customers are answered with width and are caused by length.

The cost is the number of round trips multiplied by the distance. The distance is fixed by geography and the number of round trips belongs to your product, which means the multiplier is the part you control and the part nobody measures.

And there are three fixes, in ascending order of cost and descending order of how often they are proposed: make fewer round trips, keep the unchanging things in many cities, or run the whole product closer to the customer. Companies reach for the third first.

Next is the ledger from chapter two, the small clever thing that records what exists. It is where the read replica sentence from that architecture review finally gets answered, using the wedding photograph.

## The Monday question

Ask how many round trips your busiest page makes, and how far away your most distant significant customer is. If nobody has measured the first, that is the finding, and it is the cheapest item on the list to fix as well as the one nobody will propose.
