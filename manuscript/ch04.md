# Chapter 4: Compute, Renting Brains

"Can we just make it bigger?"

The head of sales asked it in the way people ask questions they believe are simple. The product was slow, a large customer had complained twice, and the fix seemed obvious to everyone who did not have to implement it. Make the servers bigger. Pay more. Move on.

The engineering lead said yes. Then he said it depends. Then he started a sentence about the write path and stopped, because he could see he had lost the room in under ten seconds.

Nobody in that meeting was being difficult. The honest answer was that half of what needed to get bigger could be bigger by Thursday, and the other half was a piece of work somebody would have to plan, staff, and test for most of a quarter. Both halves were true at once, and the room had no way to hold both, so it heard "it depends" and translated it, as rooms do, into engineering being evasive.

That translation costs companies more than any technical decision in this book. The question was reasonable. The answer was reasonable. What was missing was a shared picture of what a server is when you are renting it, and why some kinds of bigger arrive in an afternoon while others arrive in a quarter.

## The business problem

You are going to hear "we need bigger instances" in a meeting, and you need to sort it into one of two piles within about a minute.

The first pile is a change somebody makes on Thursday afternoon. Somebody picks a larger size from a list, restarts something during a quiet hour, and the bill goes up from that day forward. There is a risk, it is small and understood, and the meeting can move on.

The second pile is a project. It has a design, a migration, a period where two things run at once, and a real chance of a bad week in the middle. It costs a quarter of somebody's roadmap, and if it gets approved in the same breath as the first pile, everyone will be surprised in six weeks when it is not done.

Both piles are described with the same sentence in the same tone by people who are not trying to mislead you. Telling them apart is the single most useful thing this chapter can give you, and it does not require you to understand a line of code. It requires one distinction, which the rest of this chapter builds.

Then there is the bill, which moves in different directions depending on which pile you chose, and in a way that surprises finance about a quarter later.

## What you are renting

Start with the thing itself, because "server" has become a word people use without a picture attached.

When your company rents compute, it is renting a working machine by the hour: processors, memory, a disk, a network connection. The industry calls one of these an **instance**, which is a forgettable word for a memorable idea. You are renting brains by the hour. The machine exists when you ask for it, does work, and costs you money for every hour it stays switched on, whether it is busy or idle.

The landlord offers these in sizes, the way a van hire company offers vans. Small ones for small jobs. Enormous ones with a great deal of memory for jobs that need to hold a lot of things in mind at once. You pick a size, you pay for that size, and if you picked wrong you can pick again.

Being able to pick again is the part that would have astonished anyone running the room in chapter one. Choosing the wrong size of machine used to be a mistake you lived with for the depreciation schedule, which is why so much effort went into choosing correctly and why the choosing took months. Now it is a decision with an undo button, and the undo takes an afternoon. Whole categories of careful advance planning stopped being worth doing, and a generation of people who were good at that planning had to find something else to be good at.

Two things about this arrangement matter to a business reader, and neither is technical.

The first is that idle machines cost the same as busy ones. A rented machine running at a tenth of its capacity bills identically to one running flat out. This is the ghost of chapter one's problem, moved indoors: you are still paying for the busiest minute, except now you can change your mind in an afternoon instead of eighteen months.

The second is the nine-second worker from chapter two. When the wedding photo arrived and something had to make the small preview, a machine woke up, did that job, and stopped. Nobody bought it. Nobody sized it for the worst Saturday of the year. It existed for nine seconds and then stopped costing anything. Hold both pictures at once, because they are the two ends of this chapter: a machine you rent by the month and leave running, and a machine that exists for nine seconds. Same idea, opposite economics, and most companies run both without anyone drawing the distinction out loud.

## Bigger, or more?

Here is the distinction that sorts the two piles.

There are two ways to give a system more capacity, and they are not variations on a theme. They are different in kind.

**Bigger** means replacing the machine with a larger one. More processors, more memory, same single machine doing the same job. In van terms, you swap the transit for a lorry.

**More** means running additional copies of the same machine alongside each other, and having the front door from chapter two share the work between them. Same size van, five of them.

The van comparison holds further than it has any right to. A delivery company with one lorry can carry more per trip than a company with one van, and it still has one driver, one breakdown, and one route at a time. A company with five vans can be in five places at once, keep running when one is in the garage, and hire a sixth for December. The second arrangement is more capable and more complicated, and the complication is the price of admission.

Bigger is easy. It is a dropdown, a restart during a quiet hour, and a higher bill. That is the Thursday afternoon pile, and it is why the honest answer to "can we just make it bigger" often is yes.

Bigger also has three limits, which is why the answer is not always yes. There is a largest size the landlord sells, and once you are on it there is no next step. The price does not rise in a straight line; the biggest machines cost more per unit of work than the middling ones, because scarcity has a price. And a single machine, however enormous, is still a single machine, so when it fails it takes everything with it. You have bought capacity and no resilience.

More is harder, and this is where the quarter goes. Running five copies of something works only if the thing was built to be copied. That means no copy can be holding something the others need. If one machine is keeping a running total in its own memory, or holding a customer's half-finished session, or writing to a file only it can see, then a second copy does not help. It creates a second version of the truth, which is worse than being slow.

So the question underneath "can we just make it bigger" is: **is this thing copyable, or does it hold something only it knows?**

If copyable, the answer is hours. If not, the answer is a project, because somebody has to move that held thing out of the machine's memory and into a place all copies can share, and then prove nothing broke. That is not a small piece of work and it is not optional, which is why the engineering lead said yes and then said it depends.

It helps to know what the held thing usually turns out to be, because it is rarely exotic. It is the customer's logged-in session, so that moving them to a second copy would log them out. It is a queue of jobs waiting to be processed, sitting in one machine's memory, invisible to any other. It is a scheduled task that must run once an hour and would run five times an hour if five copies each believed it was their job. It is a file that gets written to local disk and read back later by the same machine, which works flawlessly until a second machine looks for a file that was never on its disk.

Each of those has a known fix, and each fix is a piece of work with a name and a duration. That is the useful part. When a team says the system cannot be copied, they are not describing a mystery. They are describing a list, and the list can be costed. A reasonable follow-up in any meeting is to ask how long that list is, because a list of two is a sprint and a list of nine is the quarter everybody feared.

## Which way the bill moves

The two piles bill differently, and the difference is worth knowing before you approve either.

Bigger raises the floor. The machine is larger from Thursday onward, and it costs the larger amount at three in the morning on a quiet Sunday, because it is switched on. You have not bought capacity for busy periods. You have bought a permanently more expensive machine, sized for your worst hour, running through all your best ones.

More can follow demand. Five copies during the day, two overnight, twelve on the worst Saturday of the year. You pay for what is running, which means the bill tracks the shape of your business rather than the shape of your fears. That is the arrangement chapter eight is about, and it is only available to systems that can be copied.

This is the argument that persuades a finance director, and it is worth having ready. The expensive path is not "more machines." The expensive path is one enormous machine sized for a peak that happens four hours a week, paid for during the other hundred and sixty-four.

There is a further wrinkle that catches people out, and it belongs here rather than in the economics chapter because it changes which pile you choose. The landlords price a committed machine lower than an uncommitted one. Promise to keep something running for a year and the hourly rate drops; keep the freedom to walk away at any moment and you pay a premium for it. That premium is the price of the freedom chapter one described, and it is now a line item rather than a philosophy.

Which means the sensible arrangement for most companies is a mixture. The capacity you know you will need all year, committed and cheaper. The capacity you need for the busy hours, uncommitted and dearer per hour, running only while it earns. Companies get this wrong in both directions: some commit to nothing and pay the premium on everything, some commit to a peak they hit twice a year and pay for it through both quiet quarters. Neither error is visible without looking, which is why nobody notices for a year.

## Why software ships weekly now

There is a second thing that changed about the machine, and it explains something Meera has noticed without being able to name.

Software used to ship in quarterly batches. It now ships several times a week, at most companies, without ceremony. That change is not down to better project management or a cultural shift toward bravery. It is down to the unit of shipping.

It used to be that a program and the machine it ran on were tangled together. The program depended on things that had been installed on that particular machine over years, by various people, some of whom had left. Moving it somewhere else meant recreating that history, and nobody could be sure they had recreated all of it. So releases were rare, large, and frightening, and the machine that ran your software was a place with a history rather than a thing you could replace.

What changed is that the unit of shipping became self-contained. A release now includes everything the program needs to run, packaged together, so that it lands identically on any machine that will take it. The machine stopped being a place with a history and became interchangeable, like a shipping container's slot on a boat.

Two consequences follow, and both are business facts rather than technical ones.

The first is cadence. If a release is self-contained and identical everywhere, deploying it is routine rather than exceptional, and routine things happen weekly instead of quarterly. When Meera's engineering team says they release on Tuesdays and Thursdays, this is why, and it is also why they can undo a bad release in minutes: they still have the previous self-contained thing, and putting it back is another routine deployment.

The second is that machines became disposable. If any machine will do, then a misbehaving machine gets replaced rather than repaired. Nobody logs in to investigate at two in the morning; something notices, throws it away, and starts another. The craft of nursing a specific machine through the night, which chapter one described dying with the server room, died here rather than at the landlord's door.

None of this requires you to learn what the packaging is called. What it buys you is the ability to hear "we can ship that Thursday" and know whether it is bravado or routine, and to ask why a release takes three weeks at a company where it should take a day.

## The specialist crane

One more kind of machine, because it is the one in the news and the one your bill will notice.

Ordinary compute is a general-purpose worker. Some jobs, particularly the mathematics behind modern artificial intelligence, run faster on a specialised chip built for a narrow kind of arithmetic. The industry calls these **GPUs**. Think of a specialist crane at a port: it does one category of work far better than anything else on site, there are few of them, they cost a great deal to run, and everybody wants one at the same time.

For a business reader, three things about them matter, and none is a number.

They are expensive relative to ordinary compute, enough that a single one left running by accident shows up on the bill in a way an ordinary machine does not. They are scarce in a way ordinary compute is not, so "we will add more next month" is a promise nobody can make on your behalf. And they punish idleness harder than anything else you rent, because the price of leaving one switched on and unused is the highest per-hour waste available to you.

That is the cost shape. The practical consequence for a business reader is that any plan involving these machines needs two questions the ordinary compute plan does not: can we get them, and what happens to the bill if the job takes longer than expected. Neither question has a comfortable answer, and both are better asked at the start of a project than during it.

The reason this became an industry-wide scramble, and what it did to the landlords, is the closing chapter of this book.

## What Meera does with this

Meera does not need to know what her product holds in memory. She needs to ask one question and listen to the shape of the answer.

When somebody says the system needs bigger machines, she asks: **"Is this a size change, or do we need to run more copies? And if it is more copies, what is currently stopping us?"**

If the answer is a size change, she can expect it this week and should ask what it does to the monthly bill, since that increase is permanent and arrives whether or not the customers do.

If the answer is more copies and nothing is stopping them, that is also fast, and the good news is that the bill will follow demand rather than sitting at the top.

If the answer is more copies and something is stopping them, she has found the project. The right follow-up is not technical. It is: **"What is the thing that only that machine knows, and what would it take to move it somewhere shared?"** Whoever answers will describe a piece of work with a shape, a duration, and a risk, and Meera can take that to the roadmap as a real item rather than as a mystery costing an unspecified amount of the quarter.

This is the estimate question from chapter two, arriving at a specific box. She still cannot tell whether six weeks is honest. She can tell whether the work is a dropdown or a redesign, and she can tell when those two have been quoted as one number.

The quoted-as-one-number case is worth watching for, because it is common and nobody does it on purpose. A team asked how long something will take will answer about the whole thing, including a part that is a dropdown and a part that is a redesign, and produce a single figure that hides the split. Meera's move is to ask for the two separately: what could we do this week, and what is the part that needs planning. Often the week's worth of work solves the customer complaint that started the conversation, and the quarter's worth turns out to be something the company should do this year but not this month. Those are two different decisions, and they were about to be made as one.

The second thing she can do with this chapter is read a roadmap differently. When a feature is described as needing new infrastructure, she can ask whether it needs machines that exist all the time or work that happens in bursts. A feature that runs a heavy job once a night is a different cost and a different risk from one that adds load to every request all day. Both are described as "it needs compute," and they are not the same animal.

## Inside a real organization

The pattern that catches companies is not choosing wrong. It is choosing bigger four times in a row.

Bigger is available today and requires no design work, so it wins every individual argument against a project that would take a quarter. Each time the system strains, somebody moves up a size, the problem goes away for a few months, and nobody has to schedule anything. Then one day the team is on the largest machine the landlord sells, the strain returns, and the project that would have taken a quarter is now urgent, unplanned, and being done by people under pressure. The cheap decision was cheap four times and expensive once, and the once is larger than the four.

There is a defensible version of choosing bigger four times, and it is worth stating so the pattern does not read as stupidity. A young company should choose bigger every time. The redesign costs a quarter it does not have, the ceiling is years away at its current growth, and the company may not exist long enough to hit it. Buying time with a dropdown is the correct trade when time is the scarce thing. What goes wrong is that nobody revisits the trade once the company is no longer young, because the decision was never recorded as a trade. It was recorded as a fix.

The second pattern is quieter and shows up on the bill rather than in an incident. Machines get created for a reason and outlive it. A test environment stands up for a launch and stays running for two years. A team doubles a size during a busy quarter and nobody moves it back down when the quarter ends, because nothing is broken and moving it back has a small risk attached. Nobody is careless. Every individual machine has a story. Added together they are a line on the invoice that grows without anyone deciding it should, which is the subject of chapter eleven.

## The misconception

The belief is that computing power is a dial. Turn it up when you need more, turn it down when you do not, pay accordingly.

The dial exists for one of the two piles and not the other. You can turn a machine up. What you cannot do by turning anything is make a system that was built to run in one place run in five, and that is the change most often requested with the word "just" in front of it.

The deeper version of this misconception is that scale is something you buy. Scale is something a system either permits or does not, decided years earlier by people who were not thinking about this Saturday. Money can buy a bigger machine within an afternoon. Money cannot buy the property of being copyable; that has to be built, and it is built by people, on a calendar, at the pace people work.

Which returns to chapter one's argument in a new place. The gap between how fast demand moves and how slowly capacity follows did not vanish when the room moved into the landlord's warehouse. It moved up a level. It is no longer eighteen months of concrete. It is a quarter of engineering, and it is still the reason the answer to "can we just make it bigger" is sometimes yes and sometimes a project.

## Summary

Three things to keep.

An instance is brains by the hour, and an idle one bills the same as a busy one. Two shapes exist: the machine you leave running for months, and the worker that exists for nine seconds. Most companies run both.

Bigger and more are different in kind. Bigger is a dropdown and a restart, it raises your floor permanently, and it runs out at the largest size the landlord sells. More follows demand and costs what your business costs, and it is available only if the system can be copied. The question that sorts them is whether the machine is holding something only it knows.

And releases got faster because the unit of shipping became self-contained, which also made machines disposable. Weekly releases are a consequence of that, not of courage.

## The Monday question

Next time someone proposes making something bigger, ask whether the system could run as several copies instead, and if not, what is stopping it. The answer tells you whether you are approving an afternoon or a quarter.
