# Data pipelines and analytics

Partners drop files onto an SFTP server overnight. A scheduler picks them up,
a chain of shell scripts and stored procedures reshapes them, a Hadoop cluster
does the heavy joins, and the warehouse refresh finishes around 06:00 on a good
night. On a bad night it finishes at 09:00 and the morning reports are wrong,
so somebody reruns it and the reports are late instead. The cluster is sized
for the annual peak, which means it is idle most of the year, and it is on a
hardware refresh cycle that comes due next spring.

Two things are being tested when a scenario looks like this. Whether you can
separate ingestion from processing from analysis rather than replacing the
whole chain with one product. And whether you know when the batch shape itself
is the problem.

## Ingestion

**Pub/Sub** is the decoupling layer for streaming ingest. Publishers write to a
topic without knowing who reads it; subscribers read at their own pace, and the
service absorbs the difference. It scales without provisioning, which is why it
answers so many "millions of devices" scenarios.

Delivery is at-least-once by default, which means your subscriber must tolerate
duplicates. That single property generates a family of exam answers:
idempotent processing, deduplication on a message identifier, or Dataflow's
exactly-once processing within a pipeline. An answer that assumes messages
arrive once and in order is wrong unless the design made it so.

Ordering is not free either. **Ordering keys** give you order within a key, at
the cost of throughput for that key, and they only apply when publishers set
them. Global ordering across a high-volume topic is not on offer, and a
requirement demanding it is usually a hint that ordering belongs in processing
rather than transport.

Bulk movement is a different problem. **Storage Transfer Service** moves data
over the network from another cloud, from an on-premises filesystem, or between
buckets. **Transfer Appliance** is hardware Google ships you, which you fill and
send back. Choosing between them is arithmetic, not preference: take the volume,
take the usable bandwidth after production traffic, and work out the days. When
the wire transfer runs longer than the business will wait, or longer than the
data stays current, the appliance wins. Do the sum in the exam; the numbers are
usually supplied precisely so you can.

**Datastream** does change data capture from operational databases, streaming
inserts, updates, and deletes into Google Cloud with minimal load on the source.
It is the answer when a scenario needs the warehouse to stay near-current
against a live transactional database without a nightly extract, and when the
source cannot tolerate a heavy query window.

## Processing

**Dataflow** is managed Apache Beam. One programming model covers both bounded
and unbounded data, so batch and streaming pipelines share code, and the service
handles autoscaling and work rebalancing. Its distinguishing features in exam
terms are windowing over unbounded streams, handling of late-arriving data, and
exactly-once semantics inside the pipeline. Anything describing event-time
aggregation, sessionisation, or watermarks is describing Dataflow.

**Dataproc** is managed Hadoop and Spark. Its purpose is existing jobs. When a
scenario says the team has Spark jobs, Hive queries, or a Hadoop estate to move
and wants minimal change, Dataproc is the answer, and it is the answer
specifically because rewriting into Beam is work the requirement excluded. The
architectural upgrade to sell alongside it is ephemeral clusters: create a
cluster for a job, read from and write to Cloud Storage rather than HDFS, delete
the cluster afterwards. That converts a permanently sized cluster into per-job
cost and removes the capacity-planning problem entirely.

The split is therefore not technical. Greenfield streaming and batch goes to
Dataflow. An existing Spark or Hadoop estate goes to Dataproc. Choosing
Dataproc for new work is a decision you should be able to defend with something
other than familiarity.

**Cloud Composer** is managed Apache Airflow, and it orchestrates rather than
processes. When the requirement is dependency management across many jobs,
retries, backfills, and a schedule, that is Composer, not another pipeline.
Simple event-driven triggers do not need it: an object landing in a bucket
firing a function is cheaper and simpler than an Airflow environment running
continuously.

## BigQuery

BigQuery separates storage from compute. You store tables and pay for the
bytes; you run queries and pay for the compute, either on-demand by bytes
scanned or through capacity reservations. Understanding that split explains
almost every BigQuery cost answer on the exam.

Under on-demand pricing you are billed for data scanned, so the two levers that
matter are the ones that reduce scanning. **Partitioning** splits a table by
date, ingestion time, or an integer range, and a query filtered on the
partitioning column reads only the partitions it needs. **Clustering** sorts
data within partitions by chosen columns, so filters and aggregations on those
columns touch less data. A daily-partitioned, appropriately clustered table
answers most "reduce query cost" questions, and `SELECT *` on a wide table is
the corresponding wrong behaviour.

Capacity pricing buys a committed amount of compute for predictable spend.
The decision follows workload shape: spiky, unpredictable, exploratory usage
suits on-demand; steady heavy usage from many analysts suits reservations.

**External tables** and federated queries let BigQuery read data in place from
Cloud Storage, Cloud SQL, Spanner, and elsewhere. Useful when data should not be
duplicated or when it is landing continuously in a bucket, at the cost of the
performance that native storage gives you. **Materialised views** precompute and
incrementally refresh results, which is the answer when the same expensive
aggregation runs repeatedly on a dashboard.

BigQuery ML deserves a mention here because the exam reaches for it: training a
model with SQL over data already in the warehouse, without moving it to a
separate platform. Chapter 8 covers when that is enough and when Vertex AI is
required.

## Batch against streaming

The choice is made by the requirement, not by the technology, and the tell is
almost always a sentence about freshness. Everything above condensed:

| The requirement says | Shape | Service |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A dashboard must show current data | Streaming | Pub/Sub into Dataflow, streaming into BigQuery |
| Reports refresh overnight | Batch | Scheduled Dataflow batch, or a load job |
| Events arrive late and out of order | Streaming, event time | Dataflow with windowing on event time |
| Devices buffer and upload in bursts | Streaming, tolerate duplicates | Pub/Sub, idempotent processing |
| Warehouse must track a live database | Change data capture | Datastream |
| Existing Spark or Hive jobs, no rewrite | Batch | Dataproc, ephemeral clusters |
| Many jobs with dependencies | Batch, orchestrated | Cloud Composer |
| Move a large dataset once | Neither | Storage Transfer Service, or Transfer Appliance |

Two rules survive every variation of these questions. **Raw data lands in Cloud
Storage first**, whatever the processing shape, because capture is cheap and
irreversible decisions are not. And **streaming is a requirement, not an
upgrade** — a scenario that never mentions freshness is not asking for it, and
the streaming answer is then the expensive distractor.

## Exam traps

Cheap durable capture of data whose future value is unknown means Cloud
Storage. Not a warehouse, not a running cluster, and not a database. Streaming
everything directly into BigQuery because it can be queried later is the
expensive wrong answer, and it appears often.

Existing Spark jobs that must move without rewriting point at Dataproc. A
question that offers Dataflow alongside a stated no-rewrite constraint is
testing whether you read the constraint.

Unbounded data with windowing, late arrivals, or event-time correctness points
at Dataflow, whatever else is on the list.

An idle cluster sized for peak is a cost finding, and the fix is ephemeral
clusters with storage in Cloud Storage, not a smaller permanent cluster.

Watch for requirements that a real-time dashboard read fresh data. Streaming
into BigQuery serves that; a nightly load does not, however much cheaper it is.

## Recap

- Pub/Sub decouples ingest and delivers at least once. Design for duplicates;
  ordering keys are narrow and not free
- Wire against appliance for bulk movement is arithmetic over volume, usable
  bandwidth, and how long the business will wait
- Dataflow for new batch and streaming work, Dataproc to move an existing
  Hadoop or Spark estate, Composer to orchestrate rather than process
- BigQuery costs follow bytes scanned. Partitioning and clustering are the two
  levers; reservations suit steady heavy use
- Land raw data in Cloud Storage first. Load into the warehouse what has proven
  worth loading

## Architecture drill

A supermarket chain wants to know, within the hour, when a product is selling
out faster than forecast. Tills in 300 stores write transactions to a local
server, which uploads a file every night. Head office runs a Hadoop cluster
doing basket analysis with jobs written over eight years by people who have
left. A separate team wants to keep every transaction indefinitely for a
machine-learning project that has not started yet. Store links are unreliable
and drop for hours at a time.

Decide before reading on:

1. What replaces the nightly file upload, and what property must the design
   tolerate?
2. What happens to the eight years of Hadoop jobs?
3. Where does "keep everything indefinitely" point?
4. Does the hourly requirement change the answer to question 1?

**Discussion.** The hourly requirement is what kills the nightly batch. Tills
publish to Pub/Sub, and the unreliable links mean the design must tolerate late
arrival, bursts on reconnection, and duplicates — so process on event time, not
arrival time, and make the processing idempotent. A store that reconnects at
14:00 with six hours of backlog must have its transactions counted in the hours
they happened, or the sell-out detection is wrong precisely when the network
was worst.

The Hadoop jobs move to Dataproc, because the constraint is eight years of
undocumented logic and nobody left to rewrite it. Sell the upgrade that comes
with it: ephemeral clusters reading from Cloud Storage rather than a permanent
cluster sized for the annual peak. Rewriting into Dataflow is the right long-term
answer and the wrong migration answer.

Keep-everything-indefinitely is Cloud Storage with lifecycle rules, not BigQuery.
The machine-learning project does not exist yet, which is exactly the case for
cheap durable capture: you cannot know what to model, and you can always load
into the warehouse later. Streaming every transaction into BigQuery to preserve
optionality is how a data platform's costs get away from it.
