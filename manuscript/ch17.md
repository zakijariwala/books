# Case study: the legacy monolith

## The scenario

**Brandell Insurance** underwrites commercial motor and liability policies
through a broker network. Its policy administration system was bought in 2006,
customised heavily between 2008 and 2013, and has been maintained since by a
team of four who are the only people who understand it.

The system is a monolith: a Java application server tier across six machines
behind a hardware load balancer, an Oracle database of about eleven terabytes,
and around ninety batch jobs bound to an enterprise scheduler. The jobs do
premium calculation, renewal notices, regulatory reporting, and the nightly
reconciliation with the banking file. Staff authenticate against Active
Directory. A file share, mounted by the application servers and by four other
systems including the document generation service, holds policy documents,
broker correspondence, and the daily banking files.

Everything runs on hardware in a facility leased until **31 March**. The lease
is not being renewed; the facility is being redeveloped and there is no
extension to negotiate. That date is fixed by someone outside the business.

The infrastructure team of nine has run VMware for a decade. Two members have
completed a Kubernetes course. Nobody has operated Kubernetes in production.

The overnight batch window runs 23:40 to 04:50 and cannot move. The 04:50
finish feeds the banking file submission, and the regulator's reporting deadline
depends on the same chain. The window has no slack in it.

One schema holds claims data including medical reports, which is regulated and
carries a residency obligation.

Stated requirements:

- Everything out of the facility before the lease ends.
- No increase in the batch window, and no missed banking submission.
- Claims data stays within the national territory, with demonstrable key
  control.
- Availability at least as good as today, which is one unplanned day of
  downtime in the last three years.
- The four people who understand the system are also the people who must run it
  afterwards.

## Working the problem

**The date decides the strategy, and the date is not technical.** Refactoring
the monolith into services would produce a better system. It would also take
years, require the four people who maintain the current system to build the new
one while still running the old one, and be scheduled against a deadline the
business does not control. Rehost the application tier onto Compute Engine,
replatform where it is safe and quick, and modernise afterwards from inside the
cloud where the clock is yours. An answer that proposes microservices before
31 March is wrong on delivery grounds, and the exam includes it precisely to see
whether you will choose the better architecture over the deliverable one.

**The capability constraint reinforces it.** Nine people who run VMware and
nobody who has operated Kubernetes in production. Containerising the estate
hands an unfamiliar platform to a team during the highest-risk month of their
careers. Team capability is a legitimate architectural input, and training is a
scheduled dependency with a duration, not an assumption.

**The database.** Eleven terabytes with a hard cutover deadline and no
tolerance for a long outage. An export and import over a weekend is arithmetic
you should actually do — volume against usable bandwidth against the window —
and it will not fit comfortably. Continuous replication is the answer: replicate
into the target while the source keeps running, let it catch up, and reduce the
cutover to a switch of a few minutes rather than a copy of many hours. Whether
the target is a managed service or a database on Compute Engine follows the
licensing and version constraints, and Oracle in particular is a case where
those constraints, rather than preference, decide.

**The batch window is the real risk.** Ninety jobs with a chain ending at 04:50
and no slack. The window depends on job runtimes and on the storage and network
performance underneath them, and none of that is identical after a move. Test
the full chain against production-scale data before cutover, not a sample.
Persistent Disk performance scales with provisioned size on several types, so
"the disk is slower" has a remedy — but you want to discover that in a rehearsal
in January, not at 03:00 on 1 April. Rehearsing the batch chain is a deliverable
with a date, not a task at the end of the plan.

**The file share.** Four systems mount it, and rewriting four systems to use
object storage before March is exactly the kind of scope that eats a migration.
Filestore is the answer for phase one: managed NFS, the dependency survives
unchanged, nothing is rewritten. Moving the documents to Cloud Storage with
lifecycle rules is cheaper and better, and it belongs in phase two, stated
explicitly so it does not get forgotten.

**Claims data.** Residency through organization policy constraints on resource
locations plus regional resource choices. Key control through CMEK, with the key
project separate from the data project and different administrators on each. If
the requirement extends to proving claims data cannot be copied out by an
authorised user, that is a VPC Service Controls perimeter, deployed in dry-run
first.

**Identity.** Active Directory continues to exist and continues to authenticate
staff. Federate rather than rebuild, so the joiner-mover-leaver process stays in
one place.

## The programme

Section 4 of the exam guide lives here, and Google's own learning path has no
course covering it.

Name the person who signs off the cutover. Not a committee — an individual with
the authority to accept the risk and to say no on the night. Agree **rollback
criteria** in writing before the window opens: the specific measurable
conditions under which you revert, and the latest time at which reverting is
still possible. Deciding that at 03:00 with the business on a bridge call is how
organisations end up half-migrated.

Plan waves by dependency cluster. The file share and everything mounting it move
together. The application tier and its database move together, or accept a
period of cross-link latency that the batch window cannot afford. Put something
low-risk in the first wave so the process gets debugged on a workload nobody
will lose sleep over.

Schedule the training. Two courses completed is not operational capability, and
the plan needs the team competent on the target before it carries the batch
chain, not afterwards.

And tell the business plainly that phase one leaves technical debt on purpose.
The monolith arrives as a monolith. Filestore arrives instead of object storage.
That is the correct trade against an immovable date, and saying so in advance is
the difference between a deliberate decision and an apparent failure to
modernise.

## The tradeoff you defend

The rejected option is modernisation-first: containerise the application tier,
break the batch jobs into event-driven workloads, move to a managed database,
and land in the cloud already improved.

It is better architecture and it is the wrong plan. The deadline is external and
immovable, the only people who understand the system are the only people who can
rebuild it, and the team has no production experience with the target platform.
A migration that misses 31 March does not degrade gracefully; the facility
closes. Rehost banks the date, and every improvement remains available on
Brandell's own schedule afterwards.

The second defence is Filestore. It is the more expensive long-term answer and
it is correct for phase one, because rewriting four systems' storage
dependencies is a project competing with the one that has the deadline.

## What the exam would ask

The lease expires on a fixed date set outside the business and cannot be
extended. Which strategy, and which two constraints in the scenario support it?

The batch chain ends at 04:50 with no slack. What must the plan include before
cutover, and which infrastructure property most commonly breaks the window
after a move?

Four systems mount a shared filesystem and cannot be modified before the
deadline. What is the phase one answer, and what is deferred?

Claims data carries a residency obligation and a key control requirement. Which
three mechanisms, and which one addresses copying by an authorised user?
