# Chapter 5: Storage, The Infinite Warehouse

"We'll just export it."

The head of operations said it near the end of a long meeting, in the tone people use for the easy part. The company was moving to a different analytics vendor. The new one was better, the contract was signed, and all that remained was to take eleven years of accumulated data out of the old place and put it in the new one. Somebody asked how long that would take. Two days, said the room, more or less in unison, because moving files is the sort of thing computers are known to be good at.

It took five weeks. The first attempt ran for four days and failed near the end, which meant starting again. The second attempt was throttled to a speed that would not disturb the live product, because the first attempt had disturbed the live product. And when the invoice arrived the following month, there was a line on it nobody in that meeting had known existed, large enough that the finance director asked whether it was a mistake.

It was not a mistake. It was the cost of taking the data out.

Nobody had been careless. Every person in that room had eleven years of experience telling them that storing things is cheap, because for eleven years it had been. What none of them had noticed is that cheap-to-keep and cheap-to-move are unrelated properties, and the gap between them is where a two-day estimate becomes five weeks and a bill.

## The business problem

This chapter is about a single asymmetry, and it has a shape you can carry into any meeting.

**Putting data in is cheap. Keeping it is cheaper. Getting it out costs real money.**

That sentence explains a set of business events that otherwise look unrelated: why the migration overran, why the analytics project cost more than the analytics, why leaving a vendor is harder than arriving, why the bill has a line on it that grows when you launch a feature that seems to have nothing to do with storage.

It also explains a strategic fact that the landlords have never had to say out loud. The cheapest thing they sell is holding onto your data. The expensive thing is handing it back. You do not have to believe anyone designed that deliberately to notice that it makes leaving harder every year you stay, and that the effect compounds without anyone doing anything.

For Meera the practical consequence is small and immediate. When somebody in a meeting says the words "we'll just export it," or "we can always pull that out later," or "the data is portable," she needs to know that a real cost has entered the room without a number attached to it.

The asymmetry is worth contrasting with the chapter before it, because the two behave in opposite ways and companies manage them as though they were the same thing. Compute, from chapter four, is a tap. Turn it off and the charge stops that hour. Storage is a ratchet. It goes one way, it accumulates, and turning something off does not reduce it, because the data outlives the thing that made it. A company that switches off a whole product still pays to keep what that product recorded, for as long as somebody is nervous about deleting it, which is usually forever.

## The warehouse

The photo from chapter two landed on a shelf. Stay with that shelf, because the warehouse it sits in behaves unlike anything else you rent.

The landlord's storage business is built for things that arrive and rarely leave. That is not a limitation; it is the design. It is why the price of keeping something can be so low that nobody thinks about the decision to keep it. The warehouse expects to be a place where objects come to rest.

Three properties follow, and they explain most of what surprises people.

**It is effectively bottomless.** You will not fill it. There is no moment where somebody has to approve an expansion, no eighteen-month wait for concrete, no capacity meeting. The gap from chapter one does not exist here, and it is the one part of the system where it does not. This is new: for most of computing history, running out of space was a normal Tuesday problem, and now it is not a problem at all.

**It charges by occupancy.** You pay for what is on the shelves, all the time, for as long as it sits there. Nobody sends you a bill for the decision to keep something. You are billed a small amount, monthly, forever, which is a different psychological object from a purchase and much easier to ignore.

**It charges again for the loading dock.** Data coming in is welcomed at no charge or close to it. Data going out, to another vendor, to another country, to a customer, is metered and billed. The industry word for that outbound movement is **egress**, and it is the single most surprising line on a cloud bill for people encountering one for the first time.

A warehouse is a fair way to picture this. Shelving a pallet is cheap. Storing it for a year is cheap. Loading it onto lorries and shipping it across the country is the expensive part of the whole operation, and it is expensive whether you are moving one pallet or the entire building's worth. Freight is not storage, and the freight bill is where the money is.

There is a fourth property worth naming, because it is the reason anyone tolerates the other three. **The warehouse does not lose things.** Your file is not sitting on one disk waiting for that disk to fail. The landlord keeps several copies, in separate buildings, checks them against each other, and replaces any copy that goes bad without telling you, because from your side nothing happened. The chance of the warehouse losing a file it accepted is smaller than the chance of your office burning down.

That durability is the product. It is what a company is buying when it stops keeping its own copies, and it is why the storage bill feels acceptable even when it grows. What it does not buy is protection from your own systems, which will cheerfully overwrite or delete a perfectly durable file if instructed to. The warehouse guarantees the shelf, not the wisdom of what you put on it, which is chapter three's shared responsibility line showing up in a new place.

The reason the loading dock costs money at all is worth a sentence, because it makes the whole arrangement less mysterious. Keeping a file is a fixed cost the landlord has already paid for: the building exists, the disk is spinning either way. Sending your data out means putting it on somebody else's roads, and those roads are bought, laid, and paid for by the landlord in advance. Bandwidth out of the building is a real, finite, purchased thing, and it is metered because it is scarce in a way shelf space is not.

## Shelves at different depths

The other thing the warehouse sells is depth, and this is a real business trade rather than a technical detail.

Some shelves are at the front, near the door. Anything on them can be handed over the instant it is asked for. Other shelves are deep in the building, or in an annexe, or on a pallet at the back that requires a forklift and some notice. The deeper the shelf, the less it costs to keep something there, and the more it costs, in money or in waiting, to get it back.

That is the trade in full: **cheaper to keep, dearer and slower to retrieve.** No dollar figures required. The shape is the point.

Which shelf is correct depends on one question, and it is a business question. **How likely is anyone to ask for this, and how fast will they need it?** The customer's most recent invoice should be at the front, because they may open it this afternoon. The audit records from six years ago belong at the back, because the only entity likely to ask for them is a regulator, and regulators can wait a day.

Companies get this wrong in a predictable direction. Almost everything sits at the front, because the front is the default and moving things back requires somebody to decide what is unlikely to be needed. Deciding that is uncomfortable, so it does not happen, and the company pays front-shelf rates for a warehouse mostly full of things nobody will ever request.

The trap in the deep shelves is that the retrieval cost is easy to forget when the storage saving is visible every month. A team moves several years of records to the back, watches the monthly figure drop, and feels rewarded. Then a legal request arrives requiring all of it back at once, and the retrieval costs more than the two years of savings. Nobody did anything wrong. The trade was correct on the day it was made and wrong on the one day it mattered, which is the nature of every insurance decision ever taken.

The practical guidance for a business reader is short. Deep shelves suit things that are kept for obligation rather than use: records held because a regulator requires it, archives nobody has opened, copies kept in case of disaster. Front shelves suit anything a customer might touch. The awkward middle, things that might be needed in bulk on a day you cannot predict, is where the thinking belongs, and it is a question about the business rather than the technology.

## Two photographs, two decisions

Go back to the wedding for a moment, because the upload from chapter two made two storage decisions that look identical and are not.

The original photograph is large. It will be looked at hard for a day, forwarded during the first week, and then never opened again. Not rarely. Never. It has to be kept, because it is the customer's memory and deleting it is unthinkable, and it will sit on a shelf for a decade, earning the landlord a small amount every month, and no human being will request it again.

The small preview is tiny and will be requested constantly, every time anyone scrolls past that conversation, for years.

Those are opposite objects with opposite requirements. One is enormous and cold; the other is minuscule and hot. Keeping them on the same shelf, which is what happens by default, means either paying front-shelf rates for something nobody will read or accepting a delay on something that has to appear instantly. Getting this right is worth real money at scale, and it is invisible to everyone who has not thought about the warehouse.

The ratio is what makes it worth caring about. A preview is a tiny fraction of the size of the original it represents, and it is requested thousands of times more often. So the hot object is small and the cold object is enormous, which is a fortunate arrangement: the thing you must keep at the front costs little to keep there, and the thing that dominates your storage bill is the thing nobody reads. Once a company sees that split, the decision about deep shelves becomes obvious rather than uncomfortable, because the candidates for the back of the warehouse are exactly the objects with no audience.

That is the whole discipline of storage, stated once: **know what will be read, and put the rest where reading is slower and keeping is cheaper.**

The reason nobody does this by default is that it requires a prediction about the future behaviour of customers, and predictions are somebody's neck. The engineer who moves five years of originals to a deeper shelf has saved money that nobody will thank them for and accepted a small risk that somebody will one day wait longer than they expected. That trade has no upside for the individual and a visible downside, so it does not get made. It gets made when a manager asks for it, owns it, and says out loud that the slower retrieval is acceptable.

This is a pattern worth recognising, because it repeats throughout this book. Most of the money wasted in cloud infrastructure is not wasted through incompetence. It is wasted because the saving belongs to the company and the risk belongs to a person, and no sensible person accepts that trade unprompted.

## What Meera does with this

Meera cannot audit her company's shelves, and should not try. What she can do is price a sentence before it becomes a plan.

The sentence is "we'll just export it," and its relatives: we can always migrate later, the data is portable, we'll pull a copy for the customer, we'll send it to the new vendor. Each of those describes moving data out, which is the expensive direction, and none of them sound expensive.

Her question is: **"How much data, and has anyone moved that much out of there before?"**

The first half gets her an order of magnitude, and orders of magnitude are what matter here; the difference between moving a filing cabinet and moving a warehouse is not a detail. The second half is the important one, because a team that has done it before knows about the throttling, the failed run, and the line on the invoice, and a team that has not will estimate two days.

There is a second use, and it belongs in the room where features are decided. Some product decisions quietly commit the company to moving large amounts of data out on a recurring basis, forever. An integration that ships a nightly copy of everything to a partner. A reporting feature that lets customers download their entire history. An arrangement where analytics live with a different vendor from the product. Each is a reasonable thing to build, and each converts a one-time question into a monthly line that grows with the business.

Meera's move is to ask, at the point the feature is designed rather than after it ships: **does this move data out, how often, and does the amount grow as we grow?** A feature whose cost grows with data rather than with users is a different financial animal from one that does not, and chapter eleven is about telling those apart on the bill. The cheapest moment to notice is now.

The third use is the exit. When somebody at board level asks what it would cost to leave a vendor, part of the answer is the freight bill for eleven years of accumulated data, plus the weeks it takes to move it, plus the fact that the business keeps generating more while the move is happening. That is not an argument against leaving. It is the number that makes the conversation honest.

That last point deserves emphasis, because it is the part teams discover during the migration rather than before it. Data does not hold still while you move it. The company is open, customers are uploading, and the eleven years you started copying became eleven years and five weeks by the time you finished. Every large migration therefore has a final phase where the two systems run together and the remainder is caught up, and that phase is where the risk concentrates. When a team quotes five weeks, ask what happens to the data created during those five weeks. If the answer arrives quickly, they have done this before. If it does not, the five weeks is optimistic.

There is a fourth use, and it is the one that will make her useful to a customer. Clients increasingly ask for their own data back, sometimes because a regulation entitles them to it and sometimes because they are hedging against you. That request lands as a product requirement with a storage bill attached, and it recurs. Knowing that outbound movement is the metered direction lets Meera design the feature sensibly, offering scheduled bulk exports rather than an on-demand button that a large customer could press repeatedly on a slow afternoon.

## Inside a real organization

Nobody deletes anything, and this is the thing that separates practice from theory.

In principle, a company should periodically review what it holds and remove what it no longer needs. In practice this happens close to never, and the reasons are sound. Deleting is irreversible. The person who would authorise it does not know who depends on the data. There is a small chance somebody will need it and a certainty of blame if it is gone. Keeping costs a small amount that nobody notices, and deleting carries a risk somebody would own. Faced with that trade, every rational individual keeps everything, and the aggregate is a warehouse that only grows.

The second pattern is that the warehouse fills with things nobody meant to keep. Logs written during an incident three years ago. Copies made before a risky change, kept in case the change went wrong, still there long after it went right. Exports somebody generated for a meeting. Backups of systems that no longer exist. None of it was a decision. All of it is on the invoice, at front-shelf rates, and it grows every quarter.

What makes this hard to clean up is not the deleting. It is the knowing. Faced with a large quantity of accumulated data, the first question is what it is and who relies on it, and answering that for something created by a team that has since been reorganised takes real investigation. So the cleanup gets scheduled, descoped, and rescheduled, and the safe move is to keep paying, because the payment is small and the investigation is not. The bill grows by an amount too small to justify the work required to stop it, every month, indefinitely.

The exception, and it is the only reliable one, is a deletion policy set at the start. A company that decides on day one that a certain kind of record is kept for a stated period and then removed has a rule rather than a judgement, and rules survive reorganisations. Retrofitting that policy years later means making a hundred individual judgements about existing data, which is why almost nobody does it.

There is a shape to this worth naming, because it explains why storage bills behave unlike any other line. Compute costs track what your business is doing right now: busy day, larger bill; quiet weekend, smaller one. Storage costs track everything your business has ever done. The line only rises. It does not fall when traffic drops, it does not fall when you lose a customer, and it does not fall when the feature that generated the data gets retired, because retiring a feature does not delete its history. A company can shrink and watch its storage bill continue to climb, which is a disconcerting thing to explain to a board and a fact worth knowing before you have to.

The third pattern is the one that connects to chapter three. Where the data sits is not always where anyone thinks, because copies migrate. A backup lands in a second region for safety, an analytics pipeline lands a copy with a third-party vendor, and a support tool retains whatever customers pasted into tickets. Each copy is stored somewhere, costs something, and sits under some country's law. The residency question and the storage bill turn out to be the same inventory, asked by two different departments who have never compared notes.

## The misconception

The belief is that storage is cheap, therefore data is free.

The first half is true and is what makes the second half so persuasive. Keeping a thing costs so little that no individual decision to keep it feels like spending. And it is fine, right up until the moment you want the data somewhere else, at which point you discover you were not buying a cupboard. You were renting a warehouse in someone else's building, with a loading dock they operate and meter.

The sharper version of the correction is this. What is cheap is **stillness**. Data that sits costs almost nothing. Data that moves costs money, and it costs money every single time it moves, whether it is moving to another vendor, to another country, or to a customer who clicked download.

Which reframes a phrase heard in every planning meeting. "The data is portable" is a statement about format: it can be read by other software. It is not a statement about cost or time. Data can be entirely portable and still take five weeks and a surprising invoice to move. Portability and mobility are different properties, and only one of them is free.

And it explains the strategic shape of this industry without needing anyone to be a villain. Every year you stay, the warehouse holds more, and the cost of leaving rises, and nobody sent a memo about it. Chapter three called that lock-in and counted the rewritten code. This is the other half of the number, the part that grows on its own while everyone is busy.

For a reader with no company and no bill, the same correction lands somewhere more personal. The reason a photo service will store your entire life at no charge, and the reason it is so easy to put things in and so awkward to get everything out at once, is this asymmetry seen from the other side. You are not being cheated; you are being offered the cheap direction generously and the expensive direction reluctantly. The rational response is not suspicion. It is knowing which direction is which before you have accumulated fifteen years of things in a building you do not own.

## Summary

Three things to keep.

Putting data in is cheap, keeping it is cheaper, and taking it out costs real money and real weeks. Cheap-to-keep and cheap-to-move are unrelated properties, and confusing them is what turns a two-day estimate into five weeks.

Shelves come at different depths, and the trade is always the same: cheaper to keep, dearer and slower to retrieve. The question that picks the shelf is how likely anyone is to ask for the thing, which is a business question, not a technical one.

And nobody deletes anything, for reasons that are individually rational and collectively expensive. The warehouse only grows, which means the exit cost only grows, and neither fact will ever announce itself.

## The Monday question

Next time somebody says the company will "just export" something, ask two things: how much data it is, and whether anyone here has moved that much out before. If nobody has, the estimate in the room is a guess, the timeline is optimistic, and the invoice will arrive a month after the work does.
