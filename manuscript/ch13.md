# Part II: Case studies

## Why these are not Google's case studies

The five scenarios in Part II are original. Northmoor, Wexley, Halverston,
Ardwick, and Brandell do not exist, and you will not meet them in a scored
exam. That is deliberate, for two reasons.

The first is legal. Google's case studies are copyrighted text. Reprinting them
in a commercial book is an infringement, and prep material that does it invites
a takedown that takes the rest of the book with it.

The second reason matters more to you. **Google retires case studies.**
Mountkirk Games, Helicopter Racing League, TerramEarth, and Dress4Win were all
live once, and are all gone. Any book that teaches four specific scenarios is
teaching a set with an expiry date, and a candidate who memorised those four
learned four answers rather than a method. When the set rotated, they had
nothing.

Part II trains the muscle instead. Each scenario is built to exercise the
same decisions the live set exercises — a regulated migration, two
generative-AI modernisations, connected-vehicle telemetry, a legacy monolith
against a deadline — while being immune to Google's release schedule. Work
through five of these and the sixth, whatever Google calls it and whatever
industry it describes, will feel familiar.

## The method

Three steps, applied to every scenario here and to whatever appears on your
screen on the day.

**Find the constraint sentence.** Every scenario contains one or two sentences
that eliminate half the answer options before you have compared any technology:
a vendor support matrix, a residency clause, a fixed date, an approval
obligation, a stated lack of experience with the platform in the attractive
answer. They are rarely flagged and often sit mid-paragraph, reading like
background.

**Name the services.** Only then. The decision is usually forced by the
constraint rather than argued from the merits.

**Defend the tradeoff.** Say what you rejected and why. The exam's harder
questions are built on requirements that pull against each other, and the
answer that scores is the one respecting the harder constraint while sequencing
the softer one.

Read the scenario before the answer options. Candidates who read the options
first spend their time comparing plausible-looking services instead of hunting
for the disqualifier, which is the whole game.

Each case study below ends with the question shapes it would generate. Treat
those the way you treated the drills in Part I: answer first, then read on.

# Case study: regulated healthcare leaving a co-location facility

## The scenario

**Northmoor Clinical Group** operates fourteen outpatient clinics and a
diagnostics laboratory. It holds patient records for roughly two million people
and processes laboratory results for a further eight hundred clinics that are
not its own.

Its systems run in a co-location facility under a contract that ends in
fourteen months and will not be renewed at a price the board will accept. The
estate is: Active Directory as the identity source for two thousand staff; a
SQL Server cluster holding the clinical records system, which the vendor
supports only on specific versions; a MySQL instance behind the laboratory
results portal; Redis in front of both for session and lookup caching; a
document store holding scanned referrals and consent forms, currently on an NFS
filer; and eleven containerised internal applications running on a Kubernetes
cluster the platform team built themselves.

Three clinics have imaging equipment that writes to local storage and cannot be
moved or modified. Those sites stay on-premises indefinitely.

Stated requirements:

- **The clinical records system and the laboratory portal must not be
  modified.** The vendor withdraws support for altered deployments, and the
  laboratory portal is certified under an accreditation that names its current
  version.
- Patient data must remain within the national territory, and the organisation
  must be able to demonstrate that it controls the encryption keys.
- The records system must sustain 99.9% availability during clinic hours.
- Applications in the cloud must reach the imaging systems at the three
  remaining sites with low, predictable latency.
- Monitoring and audit must cover both environments from one place, and the
  security team must be able to prove no patient data left the platform.
- The platform team of six has run Kubernetes for two years. They have never
  operated a service mesh.

## Working the problem

**Identity first.** Two thousand staff already exist in Active Directory, and
that directory continues to authenticate on-premises systems at the three
imaging sites. Rebuilding identity in the cloud creates a second source of
truth and a joiner-mover-leaver process that will diverge within a quarter.
Federate instead: synchronise identities to Cloud Identity and federate
authentication back to the existing directory, so deprovisioning in AD removes
cloud access as a side effect rather than as a separate task. Workloads use
service accounts and impersonation, not user credentials.

**The relational tiers.** The laboratory portal's MySQL instance is a
straightforward Cloud SQL target. SQL Server is the one to think about. Cloud
SQL for SQL Server is the default answer, but the constraint sentence says the
vendor supports only specific versions — so the question becomes whether those
versions are offered as a managed service. If they are, Cloud SQL. If they are
not, the workload lands on Compute Engine, and that is not a failure of the
design; it is the constraint being respected. Note the pattern: a managed
service is the better answer until a support matrix says otherwise.

**The cache and the documents.** Redis goes to Memorystore with no argument.
The scanned referrals go to Cloud Storage rather than to Filestore, because the
documents are written once and read occasionally — a lifecycle policy moving
them to a colder class after their active period is exactly the access shape
object storage is priced for. If the applications reading them cannot be
changed to speak object storage, Filestore is the fallback, and the constraint
sentence tells you which case you are in.

**The containers.** Eleven applications and a team that has run Kubernetes for
two years. GKE is correct here, and specifically regional GKE for the 99.9%
requirement, because a zonal control plane loses autoscaling and rollouts when
its zone fails. Autopilot deserves a look given the team's size. Cloud Run
would be the answer for a team without Kubernetes experience; that is not this
team, and reaching for it anyway ignores stated capability just as surely as
recommending a mesh they have never run.

**Connectivity.** The imaging sites need low, predictable latency to cloud
workloads. That wording rules out VPN over the public internet, because the
internet promises neither. Dedicated Interconnect if the sites or their
aggregation point can reach a Google colocation facility; Partner Interconnect
if they cannot. Verify current bandwidth tiers and SLA terms before committing
a number.

**The regulated data.** Residency is an organization policy constraint
restricting resource locations, plus regional rather than multi-region choices
for buckets and databases. Key control is CMEK, with the key project separate
from the data projects and different administrators on each. The requirement to
prove no patient data left the platform is not an IAM requirement — a user with
legitimate read access can copy data out and every step is authorised. That is
VPC Service Controls: a perimeter around the projects holding patient data,
deployed in dry-run first to find the legitimate flows, with narrow egress rules
for the laboratory results feed to external clinics.

**Monitoring across both environments.** Cloud Monitoring and Cloud Logging with
agents on the remaining on-premises systems, aggregated sinks at the folder
level, and export to a logging project the workload administrators cannot
modify.

## The tradeoff you defend

The obvious modernisation is to refactor the clinical records system into
containers alongside the eleven applications already running there. It would run
cheaper, scale better, and remove the largest legacy component in the estate.

It is wrong, and the sentence that makes it wrong is the vendor support and
accreditation constraint. A refactored records system is unsupported and
uncertified, which converts a technical improvement into a regulatory incident.
The correct architecture replatforms what it may and rehosts what it must, and
revisits the records system when the vendor's own roadmap allows it.

The second tradeoff is the mesh. Eleven services with mutual TLS between them
sounds like a compliance win. The team has never operated one, the migration has
a fourteen-month clock, and encryption in transit is available without it.
Complexity a team cannot operate is a reliability risk, not a security control.

## What the exam would ask

Given the vendor support matrix, where does the clinical records database run —
and what evidence in the scenario decides it?

The security team must demonstrate that patient data cannot be copied to an
external project by an authorised user. Which control, and what does IAM not do
here?

The imaging sites require predictable latency. Which connectivity option, and
which is eliminated by the word "predictable"?

The platform team is six people supporting eleven services. Which GKE mode, and
what in the scenario argues against Cloud Run despite lower operational
overhead?
