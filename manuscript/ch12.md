# Chapter 12: The AI Infrastructure Wars

The team had the model, the money, and the plan. What they did not have, and could not get, was the machines.

This was 2024. They had raised on the strength of something that worked, and they walked into the meeting expecting the easy part. You buy the computers. You have always been able to buy the computers. For fifteen years the entire promise of this industry, the promise chapter one described a generation of companies purging their server rooms to obtain, was that capacity arrived when you asked for it. Instead they were given a date. Months out. For the machines they needed, the answer was not a price. It was a waitlist, and the waitlist did not care what they had raised.

A great many capable people learned something that year which the industry had spent a decade helping everyone forget. Compute is not infinite. It never was. It looked infinite because, for a long time, demand stayed comfortably below what the warehouses could hold, and a ceiling you never touch is indistinguishable from no ceiling at all.

Then an entire industry reached for the same scarce thing in the same eighteen months, and the ceiling turned out to be where it had always been.

## The business problem

This chapter is not about artificial intelligence. It is about what happened to the infrastructure underneath it, and why that matters to somebody who will never train a model.

Three things follow from a shortage of this kind, and all three land on ordinary businesses.

**Plans that assume availability stop working.** Any roadmap containing the words "we will add more when we need to" is resting on an assumption that held for fifteen years and stopped holding. Recognising that sentence in your own plans is the practical skill this chapter offers.

**The landlords changed what they were optimising for.** From roughly 2023 onward, the companies renting you computing were competing for chips, power, and land rather than for features. That reordering shaped what got built, what got prioritised, and what became difficult to obtain, and it happened without any announcement to tenants.

**And the shape of the industry's risk changed.** Chapter nine's concentration argument sharpened: when everyone depends on a small number of landlords, and those landlords are themselves constrained by a smaller number of suppliers, the whole arrangement inherits a bottleneck that no individual company can engineer around.

A fourth thing follows for anyone selling to businesses, and it is the reason this chapter earns a place in a book that is otherwise about ordinary infrastructure. Scarcity travels. When your customers cannot get what they need, their plans slip, their budgets get redirected toward securing supply, and the project that was going to buy your product moves to next year. A shortage two layers below you in the supply chain arrives at your revenue without ever touching your systems, and it arrives without explanation unless somebody in your company is watching the layer it came from.

## The heat wave

Chapter one described a gap. Demand moves in weeks; physical capacity moves in eighteen months. That gap is the reason the cloud exists, and every chapter since has been a consequence of it.

The cloud did not close the gap. It moved it somewhere you could not see, and paid for the privilege of absorbing it on your behalf. The landlord holds the concrete, the eighteen months, and the guesswork, and sells you the illusion of instant capacity out of a buffer it maintains at enormous expense.

That arrangement works while the buffer is large relative to demand. Think of electricity, since the cloud has always invited the comparison. Flip a switch and the light comes on, and you never wonder whether there is enough. It feels infinite and instant and is neither. It is a power station somebody built years ago, sized against a guess, connected by lines that took a decade to permit. On an ordinary day nobody approaches the ceiling, so the ceiling stops being real to anyone.

Then a heat wave arrives, every air conditioner in the region starts at once, and the grid that felt infinite discovers its bottom in an afternoon. Adding more does not take an afternoon. It takes years.

Artificial intelligence was the heat wave.

The scarce thing was never money, and this is the part that surprises business readers, because money is the thing they are used to being scarce. In the build-out that began in earnest around 2023, money was abundant. What was scarce was a particular kind of chip, made in meaningful volume by one company, using manufacturing capacity that itself takes years to expand.

And beneath the chip, scarcer still, was power. A warehouse full of these machines draws electricity like a small town. You cannot plug in a small town. You need a substation, a connection to the grid, and a grid with room to spare, and by 2024 the companies building these places were shopping for megawatts as much as for real estate, in the few places where the power existed.

The rhyme with chapter one is worth sitting with, because it is the argument of this book arriving at its largest available scale. A company in 2012 wanted to double its racks and was told eighteen months, because concrete and capital approval move at the speed they move. An industry in 2024 wanted to multiply its computing capacity and was told a similar thing by physics, in a different vocabulary, for the same underlying reason. The gap was never about servers. It is what happens whenever something that can be wanted quickly depends on something that can only be built slowly.

There is one difference, and it is the reason this chapter is not chapter one repeated. A single company facing an eighteen-month wait can go elsewhere: rent, borrow, buy a competitor's spare capacity, or scale back its ambition. An entire industry facing the same wait has nowhere to go, because there is no elsewhere. When everyone reaches at once, the ordinary remedies stop working, and the only available responses are to wait, to pay for priority, or to want less.

## What the landlords did

Faced with a physical bottleneck, the landlords did what anyone does. They stopped competing on features and started competing on concrete and copper.

Through 2023, 2024, and into 2025, the race became a race to secure chips before anyone else, to lock up power years in advance, and to pour buildings at a pace the industry had never attempted. A large share of the money being raised across the sector went, in effect, toward buying a place in a queue: prepaying for capacity that would arrive in months, signing multi-year commitments for machines that did not yet exist.

Regions filled. The dropdown from chapter three, which had always offered any location you liked, began returning the digital equivalent of no vacancy for the machines everybody suddenly wanted. Requests for more capacity came back with dates instead of confirmations. There was a pecking order, and where a company sat on it decided whether its plan happened this quarter or next year.

The pecking order ran on more than politeness. Established customers with long relationships and deep commitments were served first, which is how allocation works in every industry during a shortage and is nobody's villainy. Everyone else took a number.

The part worth holding is the reversal. The landlords, who had spent a decade telling tenants that supply was endless, were now rationing it. They were not being dishonest. They had run into the same wall as everyone else, and they hit it harder, because they were the ones buying the chips and negotiating for the megawatts. The company that sold you freedom from the eighteen months discovered it had been absorbing the eighteen months all along, and that there is a volume of demand at which absorbing it stops being possible.

## What this means for a plan

Here is the use, and it requires no knowledge of chips.

In any plan that leans on the word "just," as in we will just add more when the time comes, find the physical bottleneck and ask two questions about it. **What is the one resource this depends on that cannot be bought instantly? And how long does it take to make more of that resource?**

Sometimes the answer is comfortable and you move on. Ordinary computing capacity, for ordinary workloads, remains close to the promise, and most companies will never encounter a shortage of it. Sometimes the answer is that one company makes it and the lead time is measured in quarters, and you have found the assumption that will break your roadmap while there is still time to plan around it rather than discover it on a waitlist.

The same question generalises past this industry, which is why it is the last thing this book teaches. Every business plan contains an assumption about something being available on demand: a component, a skill, a licence, a person with a particular kind of experience. Most of the time the assumption holds and nobody has to think about it. The plans that fail are the ones where somebody treated a supply chain as a switch.

There is a second, gentler use, and it protects against overcorrection. Reading a chapter like this, the temptation is to conclude that scarcity is now the normal condition and to plan defensively for everything. That would be a mistake and an expensive one. The shortage described here applies to a specific and unusual category of machine. The ordinary capacity that runs ordinary products, the machines in chapter four and the warehouses in chapter five, remained available throughout, and a company building a normal product could go on believing the promise because for that company the promise held.

The skill, then, is discrimination rather than caution. Knowing which of your dependencies is ordinary and which is scarce, and applying the lead-time question only where it belongs. A company that treats every input as constrained will move slowly and spend heavily to protect itself against a risk it does not carry, which is a way of losing to a competitor who read the situation more precisely.

The third use is the most durable and the least dramatic. When a vendor, an analyst, or a colleague tells you that some category of resource is effectively unlimited, you now know what that claim rests on: a buffer somebody maintains, sized against expected demand, which is adequate until enough people want the same thing at the same time. That is not cynicism. It is knowing where the promise comes from, which is the difference between trusting something and taking it on faith.

For Meera the specific version is a question in a roadmap meeting. When a plan depends on new infrastructure of an unusual kind, she can ask whether the company has it, has been promised it, or is assuming it will be available. Those are three different states, they are routinely described in the same sentence, and only the first is a fact.

## Inside a real organization

The pattern is that the shortage arrives as a schedule problem rather than as a technology problem, which means it lands on people who have no vocabulary for it, and it lands on them late.

A project's dependency on scarce machines is rarely stated as such at the point of approval. It appears as an assumption inside a plan, held by an engineer who knows the machines are hard to get and has no reason to think that fact belongs in a business document. The gap between what the engineering team knows about supply and what the executive team believes about supply can persist for a full quarter, and it closes on the day somebody asks why the date moved again.

A project is approved on the understanding that the machines will be there. They are not, or they are there in smaller numbers, or they are available in a region that is inconvenient for reasons chapters three and six explained. The project does not fail; it slips, repeatedly, in increments that each seem reasonable, and the explanation involves supply. Executives who have spent fifteen years being told that infrastructure is instant hear the explanation as an excuse, because for fifteen years it usually was.

The second pattern is that the shortage rewarded companies that had made an unglamorous decision years earlier: measuring what they needed. A company that understands its own workload can size a request to what it will use, commit to it, and become a customer a landlord wants to allocate to. A company that has never measured asks for a generous number based on fear, gets a fraction of it, and manages the fraction badly. Chapter eleven's discipline turned out to be a supply strategy, which nobody had intended it to be.

The third is that scarcity made the old skills briefly valuable again. Capacity forecasting, the craft chapter four described dying quietly when a dropdown replaced it, became relevant to the small number of companies working at the frontier. Not for everyone, and not permanently. But the people who had spent careers guessing how much a business would need in eighteen months found themselves in rooms again, which is a satisfying thing to watch happen.

The fourth pattern is what the shortage did to how deals were struck. A landlord with more demand than supply is in a different negotiating position from one competing for your business, and tenants felt it in ways that had nothing to do with the machines they could not get. Longer commitments in exchange for allocation. Less flexibility on terms. The freedom chapter one described buying, the freedom to walk away, is worth less in a market where walking away means joining a queue somewhere else. Nobody wrote that down. It showed up as contracts that ran longer than the previous ones.

The fifth is the one worth watching for, because it is unresolved as of 2026 and a reader will meet it. Enormous sums were committed to buildings, chips, and power on the strength of forecasts about demand that had no precedent behind them. If those forecasts hold, the capacity gets used and the scramble looks like foresight. If they overshoot, the industry ends up holding a great deal of expensive, specialised, power-hungry capacity that somebody has to find a use for, and the shortage becomes a glut. Both outcomes have been confidently predicted by serious people. This book has no idea which will happen, and any book that claims to should be read with suspicion.

## The misconception

The belief is the most expensive one in this book, and it hides inside a phrase everyone says without noticing.

**"We'll just scale up when we need to."**

People say it in planning meetings the way they say the sun will rise. It treats capacity as weather, present whenever you turn to look. For years, for most workloads, it was close enough to true that saying it cost nothing.

The AI era is the proof that it was never true. It was a loan taken against a ceiling nobody was reaching, and the loan came due the moment enough people reached for the same scarce thing at once.

The vendors will not lead with this. Their material still says elastic and on demand and available in minutes, and for the ordinary workload those words remain broadly honest. Strip the language back and the truth underneath is physical and slow. Somewhere there is a fabrication plant that took years to build. Somewhere there is a power station. Somewhere there is a field with cranes in it and a substation going up next door, and that field is the reason your plan either happens on time or does not.

The cloud never abolished the physical world. It rented it to you by the hour and hoped you would not ask where it was.

There is a second misconception that this period exposed, and it belongs to the people who read about it rather than to the people who lived it. It is that the shortage was a story about artificial intelligence. It was a story about electricity, land, and manufacturing capacity, wearing the clothes of a story about software. The reason the coverage focused on models and chips is that models and chips are legible and photogenic; substations are not. But the constraint that decided where these buildings went, and how fast, was the same one that decides where an aluminium smelter goes, and it has been decided that way for a century.

Which produces the version of this chapter a reader can carry into any industry. When a new technology arrives and everyone agrees it changes everything, the question worth asking is not what it can do. It is what it consumes, where that comes from, and how quickly more of it can be made. That question has embarrassed a great many confident forecasts, and it is available to anyone, requiring no expertise in the technology at all.

## What this book was about

Which returns us to a room in a business park off a ring road.

The pizza boxes are gone. The lease expired, the racks went to recycling, and the person who kept a fleece on the back of their chair does something else now. What replaced that room was not an absence. It was a warehouse the size of a shopping mall, in a country you did not choose from a list, run by a landlord whose phone rings instead of yours.

Everything in this book followed from one gap: demand moves in weeks, physical capacity moves in years, and no amount of money closes the distance. The cloud was the arrangement the industry built to live inside that gap. Renting instead of owning, so that being wrong about your size costs a month rather than a decade. A supply chain of six businesses rather than one room, each metering you, each able to fail on its own. A landlord whose building stands in a country with a government and a courthouse. Storage that is cheap to fill and costly to empty. Roads with a speed limit set by the size of the planet. Copies that answer questions and are a moment behind. Capacity that appears when the traffic does, unless the traffic arrives faster than the capacity can. Compartments rather than walls, because there is no longer an inside. And a bill that describes, monthly and without flattery, exactly what your product does.

None of that was designed by anyone. It accumulated, the way everything in this book accumulates, one reasonable decision at a time, and the result is a set of arrangements that would be difficult to defend if anyone had proposed them all at once and is entirely sensible taken one step at a time. That is worth knowing about any large system, including the ones you work inside. Nobody chose it. It arrived.

And at the end, an entire industry discovering the gap again, at a scale where the buffer that had absorbed it for fifteen years was not large enough.

Meera will not build any of this. She will sit in the second-Tuesday meeting, and when the platform lead says that the write path moves behind a queue and the read replicas take the load, she will know that a product decision has just been taken without being named, and she will ask who sees stale data. When somebody says the company should self-host to save money, she will ask what freedom the company would be buying back. When a customer asks where their data lives, she will answer without looking at anyone. When a distant customer says the product is slow and cannot be reproduced, she will ask how many round trips the page makes. When a bill rises by forty percent, she will ask which line changed and what decision changed it. And when a plan says the company will just add more when the time comes, she will ask what the lead time is on the thing it depends on.

None of those require her to open a terminal. Not one of them is a technical contribution. All of them change the meeting, which was the point.

The column of notes in the back of her notebook is still there, and it will keep growing, because nobody reaches the end of this subject and there is always another phrase. What has changed is that the notes are now questions rather than admissions. A term she does not recognise is no longer evidence that she does not belong in the room. It is the next thing to ask about, in front of everyone, in a meeting where asking has been established as the useful thing to do.

The wedding photograph, by the way, is still on a shelf. Nobody has looked at it since the week it was taken. It will sit there for a decade, costing a small amount every month, in a building in a country, under a government, waiting for a request that will not come.

The guest who took it will never think about any of this, and there is no reason she should. That is what a working system earns: the right to be ignored by the people it serves. The wall socket does not explain the grid, the tap does not explain the reservoir, and the share button does not explain the six businesses that carry a photograph across a continent in two seconds. Infrastructure succeeds by becoming invisible, and every chapter of this book has been an argument that invisible is not the same as absent.

Which is the whole of it. Somebody built the room. Somebody signed the lease on a building in a country you did not choose. Somebody is on call tonight for a warehouse you will never enter, and somebody is waiting on a substation that will decide whether a project happens this year. Your data is in a place. It has a cost, an owner, a jurisdiction, and a way of failing.

That is where your data lives, and now you can picture it.

## The Monday question

Take the plan you are most confident about, and find the sentence where something is assumed to be available when you reach for it. Then ask two things: how long it takes to make more of that thing, and whether the company has it, has been promised it, or is assuming it. Those three states get described in the same sentence, and only the first is a fact. If nobody can tell you which one applies, you have found the part of the plan that is a guess wearing the clothes of a commitment.
