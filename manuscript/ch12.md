# Operations, automation, and cost

The environments were built by hand, eighteen months apart, by different
people. Production and staging differ in ways nobody has enumerated, which is
why staging passes and production does not. The runbook lives in a wiki page
last edited by someone who has left, and it references a server name that no
longer resolves. The cloud bill arrives monthly, it is going up, and when
finance asks which team owns the increase the honest answer is that nobody can
tell them.

Every one of those is an architecture problem rather than an operations
problem, which is the framing the exam takes. Configuration drift is what you
get without infrastructure as code. Unattributable cost is what you get without
a project and label structure designed for attribution.

## Infrastructure as code

Terraform is the default on Google Cloud, and the exam treats it as such. Config
Controller offers a Kubernetes-style reconciliation model for teams already
committed to that approach; Deployment Manager is legacy and should not appear
in a new design.

**State placement is a design decision, not a detail.** Remote state in a Cloud
Storage bucket, with versioning enabled, gives you locking, shared access, and
recovery from a corrupted apply. Local state on an engineer's laptop means
exactly one person can change infrastructure and nobody can review what changed.
The state file also contains sensitive values, so the bucket needs the same
access control you would give a secrets store, in a project separate from the
workloads it describes.

Authenticate the pipeline with **service account impersonation** or Workload
Identity Federation, never an exported key file. This is the same principle as
chapters 2 and 5: no long-lived credential material anywhere. A CI system
holding a downloadable service account key is the finding, and storing that key
more carefully is not the fix.

Scope the Terraform service account rather than leaving it as Editor across the
organisation. Modules give you reuse across environments, and separate state per
environment gives you a blast radius. When a question describes staging and
production diverging, the answer is one module applied with different variables,
not two hand-maintained copies.

Replace a specific resource with `terraform apply -replace=ADDRESS`. The
`taint` command is deprecated, and prep material still teaching it is out of
date.

## Delivery

**Cloud Build** runs the pipeline, **Artifact Registry** stores images and
packages, and **Cloud Deploy** manages promotion through environments with
approvals between them. The approval gate is often the exam's point: a
requirement that a human authorises production release is met by the delivery
tool, not by a policy document.

**Binary Authorization** enforces at deploy time that only images meeting your
policy run — signed by a required attestor, built by your pipeline, scanned
without critical vulnerabilities. It is the answer to "prevent unapproved
images reaching production", and it is a supply chain control rather than a
scanning tool. Scanning finds the vulnerability; Binary Authorization stops the
deployment.

Three deployment strategies and the requirements that pick them. **Rolling
updates** replace instances gradually, which is the managed instance group and
Kubernetes default and the right answer for large fleets needing frequent
updates with a workable rollback. **Blue-green** stands up a complete second
environment and switches traffic, giving the fastest rollback available — switch
back. **Canary** sends a small traffic percentage to the new version and
watches metrics before proceeding, which is what you want when the risk is
behavioural rather than structural.

Blue-green has a specific failure mode worth carrying into the exam: it does not
suit workloads holding long-running transactions across the cutover. Switching
traffic abandons in-flight work on the old side, and a scenario mentioning long
transactions or long-lived sessions is disqualifying blue-green even while
praising fast rollback.

## Cost

No official course covers cost, and every case study raises it. That gap makes
this section worth over-preparing.

Three pricing mechanisms, three workload shapes. **Committed use discounts** buy
a lower rate against a one or three year commitment, and they fit a predictable
steady baseline. **Sustained use discounts** apply automatically to instances
running most of a month, so they reward steady usage without a commitment.
**Spot** capacity is deeply discounted and reclaimable, fitting interruptible
batch. The common design uses commitments for the baseline and autoscaling on
top, with Spot for the batch tier.

Rightsizing recommendations surface over-provisioned instances from observed
utilisation, and the Recommender surface carries similar findings for idle
resources, unattached disks, and over-broad IAM. When a scenario asks how to
find waste without a manual audit, that is the mechanism.

**Billing export to BigQuery** is how cost becomes analysable. Budgets with
alerts notify against a threshold, and it is worth being precise: a budget alert
notifies, it does not cap spend. A scenario expecting spending to stop
automatically needs the alert wired to an automated action, and answers that
assume budgets enforce a limit are wrong.

Attribution is structural. **Labels** on resources and a project structure that
maps to teams and workloads are what make a bill answerable; no reporting tool
recovers attribution that the resource layout never encoded. This is where
chapter 2's advice about treating projects as cheap boundaries pays off, and
where a label policy enforced through organization policy stops the structure
degrading.

The framing exam guide section 4 names is **capital expenditure against
operational expenditure**. On-premises is capital: buy the peak, depreciate it,
own the risk of guessing wrong. Cloud is operational: pay for what runs,
convert the guess into a monthly variable. When a question asks for the business
argument rather than the technical one, that shift, plus the elimination of the
refresh cycle, is the answer the exam wants.

## Exam traps

A predictable steady workload with a multi-year horizon is committed use
discounts. Spot is wrong for it no matter how much cheaper the headline rate is.

Interruption-tolerant batch is Spot, and answers proposing commitments for
bursty work are paying for capacity that is idle most of the month.

Cost attribution problems trace back to project and label structure, not to a
reporting tool. The fix is upstream of the dashboard.

Budgets alert; they do not cap. Read carefully whether the requirement is
notification or enforcement.

An exported service account key in a pipeline is the finding, and secure
storage of that key is not the remedy.

Blue-green offered against a workload with long-running transactions is a
distractor built from a correct-sounding advantage.

## Recap

- Terraform with remote state in a versioned bucket, scoped credentials through
  impersonation, one module applied per environment
- Cloud Deploy carries the approval gate; Binary Authorization enforces what
  may run, which scanning alone does not
- Rolling for fleets, canary for behavioural risk, blue-green for fast rollback
  except where long transactions cross the cutover
- Commitments for baseline, sustained use for steady running, Spot for
  interruptible batch
- Attribution is designed into projects and labels before it is reported;
  budgets notify rather than enforce
