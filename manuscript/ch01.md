# Reading the exam

Most people who fail this exam know the services. They fail because they answer
the question in front of them instead of the question the case study asked.

A typical item gives you four options. Two are wrong on the technology. The
remaining two both work. One of them costs more, or breaks a constraint stated
three paragraphs earlier in a document you last read forty minutes ago. Picking
between those two is the whole exam.

## What the guide says

Google publishes an exam guide listing six sections and their weights.

| Section | Weight |
| --- | --- |
| Designing and planning a cloud solution architecture | ~25% |
| Managing and provisioning a cloud solution infrastructure | ~17.5% |
| Designing for security and compliance | ~17.5% |
| Analyzing and optimizing technical and business processes | ~15% |
| Managing implementation | ~12.5% |
| Ensuring solution and operations excellence | ~12.5% |

Read the weights as a study budget. Design and planning takes a quarter of the
exam on its own, and it rewards judgment rather than recall. Sections 4 and 5
together take another 27.5 per cent, and both lean on process questions:
stakeholder management, change management, team readiness, cost against
CapEx and OpEx.

Those process sections trip up strong engineers. Google's own learning path
barely touches them. You will meet questions about how to run a migration
programme, not just how to build one, and the correct answer often involves
talking to somebody rather than deploying something.

## The case studies

Every sitting includes two case studies, displayed beside the questions on a
split screen. Between a fifth and a third of your questions attach to them.

The four in current use:

- **Altostrat Media**, a media company adopting generative AI
- **Cymbal Retail**, a hybrid retailer with a sprawl of database technology
- **EHR Healthcare**, a regulated healthcare provider leaving a co-location
  facility
- **KnightMotives Automotive**, an automotive scenario

Read all four before the exam. Google hosts them as PDFs linked from the exam
guide. They run a few pages each and they repay a slow read.

Two of the four centre on generative AI. That tells you where Google has moved
the emphasis, and it explains why an exam once dominated by lift-and-shift now
asks how you would put a human in the loop on a model's output.

## Find the constraint sentence

Every case study buries its answer key in one or two sentences of business
prose. The technology sections describe what the company runs. The constraint
sentence tells you what it refuses to change.

EHR Healthcare states that its existing stack stays as it is. The move
replaces a co-location facility and nothing more. That single commitment kills
every answer proposing a rewrite into serverless, however elegant. You are
replatforming.

Train yourself to locate that sentence first. Before you read a single
question, find the requirement that eliminates options, and write it on your
whiteboard. Common shapes:

- "The current stack must remain unchanged"
- "Data may not leave the country"
- "Transactions must be strongly consistent"
- "A human must approve every published change"

Each one deletes two of the four options before you have read them.

## How the wrong answers are built

Google constructs distractors from a small set of patterns. Learning them buys
you time.

**The technically correct answer that ignores the constraint.** Streaming
telemetry into BigQuery works. It also costs a fortune when the requirement
said capture everything cheaply for unknown future use, which describes Cloud
Storage.

**The right service at the wrong scope.** Cloud SQL and Spanner both hold
relational data. Only one of them survives a requirement for strong consistency
across regions.

**The answer that solves a problem nobody has.** Multi-region everything, for a
company whose users sit in one country and whose stated concern was cost.

**The technology answer to a process question.** Asked how to respond to a one
per cent failure rate last week, the exam wants root cause analysis, not a
rebuild. Section 4 exists to test whether you reach for the runbook or the
rewrite.

## Sitting the exam

Read both case studies before you touch a question. You lose less time
absorbing them once than swapping context twenty times.

Flag anything that takes more than ninety seconds and move. Case study
questions cluster, and later ones sometimes clarify earlier ones.

When two options survive your first pass, go back to the constraint sentence
rather than reasoning about the technology. The technology rarely separates
them. The business requirement does.

## What to do next

Read the four case studies end to end. For each, write down the constraint
sentence and the two or three services you would reach for. Keep those notes
beside you as you work through the rest of this book, and revise them when a
chapter changes your mind.

The chapters that follow work through the decisions those case studies demand,
starting with the one construct in Google Cloud that has no equivalent in AWS
or Azure, and that everything else depends on.
