# Chapter 1: The Great Pizza Box Purge

The servers were called pizza boxes, because that is what they looked like. Flat, black, stacked one on the next, forty to a rack. A room full of them in a business park off the ring road, behind a door that took two cards to open, cooled to the point where the night staff kept a fleece on the back of the chair. When you bought a book at nine in the evening, your order landed on one of those boxes. It had a serial number. Somebody's name was on the lease for the room, and somebody else's phone rang when the cooling failed.

If you have never stood in one of these rooms, the first thing that hits you is the noise. Thousands of small fans running at once make a flat, constant roar, the sound of a plane you never take off in. The second thing is the cold, pushed up through a raised floor so the machines can breathe chilled air from below. You walk down an aisle between two walls of blinking lights, and somewhere under your feet run the cables carrying, among a great many other things, the fact that you just bought a book. It is an oddly unimpressive place to keep something so important. That was always part of the point.

The room cost the same whether anyone bought a book or not. That is the first thing nobody tells you about owning your own computers. You pay for the busiest minute of the year, every minute of the year. On a normal Tuesday the boxes ran at a fraction of what they could hold, warm and bored, drawing power to do very little. The company had bought for the one Saturday in November when the whole country shopped at once, and then paid to keep that Saturday alive for the other three hundred and sixty-four days.

For years, this was simply what having a business online meant. You wanted to sell things to people through a screen, so you bought computers, and computers have to live somewhere, so you rented a room, and the room needed power and cooling and a person who understood both. None of it felt like a choice. It felt like the weather. Rooms full of pizza boxes were what the internet was made of, and the people who ran them mostly kept them running without anyone upstairs thinking about it at all.

The person who ran that room could tell you the age of every rack and which one ran hot, and knew the building's power draw the way a ship's engineer knows the engine room. To the executives three floors up, the room was a line in the budget called "hosting." To the people who ran it, it was a living thing with moods, and keeping it alive was a craft nobody noticed until the night it stopped.

Then something changed, and it did not change in the room. It changed in the market the room was serving.

## The problem nobody could buy their way out of

Sales doubled in a year. Good news, the kind a board celebrates with a slide and a round of applause. It was also the moment the room stopped working.

Doubling the boxes meant doubling the room. Doubling the room meant a bigger lease, a heavier power feed, more cooling, and a capital request that went up three levels and came back eighteen months later. The demand had arrived in a quarter. The answer to it moved in a year and a half.

Sit with those two speeds, because the distance between them is the reason this whole book exists. Customers change their minds in weeks. A building changes in years. You can want more capacity on Monday and be told, correctly and by people doing their jobs well, that you can have it a year from next spring, after the electrical work, after the permits, after the capital committee meets again. Money does not fix this. The company had the money. What it did not have was a way to make concrete cure faster or a substation appear on a schedule that matched a sales chart.

That gap, between how fast demand moves and how slowly capacity moves, is not a technology problem. It is a physics-and-paperwork problem, and it existed long before anyone said the word cloud out loud. Every business that has ever grown faster than it could build has lived inside that gap. The bakery that could sell twice the bread if it had a second oven, and cannot get the oven installed for six months. The airline that holds gates it does not need in February so it will have them in July. The hotel that turns away a convention because the tower it is building will not open until next year. The gap is old, and every industry has its own version of it.

What makes an online business feel the gap more sharply than a bakery is that its demand does not rise on a gentle curve. It spikes. A product gets mentioned by the right person and traffic goes up tenfold in an hour. A marketing campaign lands. A competitor goes down and their customers arrive all at once. The November rush is only the predictable spike; the dangerous ones are the spikes nobody put on the calendar. A bakery can watch demand build over a season. An online store can watch it arrive over a lunch break. Owning the room means either building for the biggest spike you can imagine and paying for that headroom every quiet day, or building for the normal day and falling over the first time you get lucky. Neither is a good place to stand.

What was new, around the turn of the 2010s, was that somebody figured out how to rent your way across the gap instead of building your way across it.

## What renting actually buys

Here is the idea that clears up most of the confusion business people carry about the cloud, and it has nothing to do with computers.

You did not buy a house. You rented from a landlord.

When a company "moves to the cloud," it stops owning the room. It stops holding the lease, the boxes, the cooling contract, and the pager. Instead it rents computers by the hour from a very large landlord who owns warehouses full of them, and pays every month for exactly what it used. When demand doubles, it asks for more and gets it this afternoon. When demand falls back after November, it hands the extra capacity back that night and stops paying for it. The eighteen-month wait for concrete becomes a dropdown menu and a slightly larger invoice.

The closest thing most people already understand is the utility. You do not build a power plant in your basement so you can run the kettle. You draw electricity from the wall, you pay for what the meter says you used, and when you unplug, the charge stops. You have never once worried about whether there is enough electricity for your kettle, and you have never paid for a power station to sit idle on a slow day. Renting computers by the hour turned capacity into that: a thing you draw when you need it and stop paying for when you do not, metered, billed after the fact, someone else's job to keep running.

Now, the trap. People hear "rent instead of own" and immediately ask whether renting is cheaper, because that is the question renting usually raises. And the honest answer is often no. A hotel room costs more per night than a mortgage costs per night. Anyone who has expensed a week of hotels knows that renting a place to sleep, night by night, is a spectacularly expensive way to be housed if you are going to stay in one place for ten years.

So if renting is not cheaper, why did nearly every company on earth do it?

Because you are not paying for the machine. You are paying for the right to give it back. What renting buys is not a lower price. It is the ability to be wrong about how much you need, in either direction, without it becoming a capital disaster. Own the room and guess too high, and you are heating empty racks for a decade. Own the room and guess too low, and you are turning away the November rush and waiting eighteen months to fix it. Rent, and being wrong costs you a bigger bill for one month, or a smaller one. You have traded a price for a freedom, and for a business whose demand jumps around, that freedom is worth more than the price.

Picture the next November under the new arrangement. The rush arrives, the same rush that used to require an eighteen-month capital plan and a prayer. This time someone changes a setting on a Tuesday, the rented capacity grows to meet the crowd, the weekend passes, and on Monday the setting goes back and the bill returns to normal. Nobody poured concrete. Nobody stood in front of a committee. The thing that used to be an existential planning problem became an operating expense that went up for a few days and came back down. That, and not any saving on the per-hour price, is what companies actually bought.

There is a fair question hiding here. How can a landlord let everyone grow on demand when it could not let you own on demand? The answer is that the landlord is not renting to you alone. It rents to thousands of businesses at once, and their busy moments do not all fall on the same day. Your quiet Tuesday is someone else's launch. Your evening lull is when the streaming service fills up, and your slow summer is the accounting firm's busiest season. Pooled together, all those spikes and lulls average into something far smoother than any single tenant's demand, and the landlord builds for the smooth aggregate plus a margin rather than for the sum of everyone's worst case. It is the same reason the power grid works. Not everyone boils the kettle at the same second, so the utility never has to build for the moment they might. When the spikes do line up, and once in a while they do, even the largest landlords can run short. We will meet that problem again at the end of this book, when an entire industry wanted the same scarce machines in the same year.

That is the trade at the center of everything that follows. Not "the cloud is cheaper." The cloud lets you match what you pay to what you actually use, in a world where you cannot predict what you will use.

## The purge

Knowing all this, companies did the obvious thing, and they did it in a rush that ran for most of a decade. They powered down the pizza boxes. They let the lease on the room off the ring road expire. They moved the whole operation onto machines they would never see, in buildings they would never enter, owned by companies that rent computing the way the utility sells power.

The people who did this work talk about it the way people talk about clearing out a parent's garage. Relief, mostly, that the thing they had maintained through a hundred late nights was now someone else's problem. A little grief, because they knew that room better than they knew some of their colleagues. And, reliably, the discovery of something load-bearing that nobody remembered plugging in: a small server under a desk running a report the finance team had depended on for six years, a cable whose purpose no one could name but no one dared to pull. Every purge turns up at least one of these. The room had accumulated history, and history does not always write itself down.

This is the Great Pizza Box Purge, and it was not one company's story. It was an entire industry deciding, more or less at once, that owning the room was a burden rather than an asset, and handing the burden to a landlord. The boxes went to recycling by the truckload. The rooms became ordinary office space, or nothing at all. And a generation of people whose job had been to keep a specific room cold found that the job had moved somewhere they could not follow.

It is worth pausing on how strange this was as a business decision, because it ran against an instinct as old as commerce: own your means of production. Factories owned their machines. Shops owned their shelves. For most of the industrial age, control meant ownership, and handing a core function to an outside party looked like weakness. The purge inverted that. Companies decided that owning the computers their business ran on was not control at all, but a liability, a room full of depreciating metal and a standing obligation to keep it cold. Control, they decided, was the ability to grow and shrink on command. Ownership was the thing standing in the way of it.

## The meeting where this comes up

Meera runs product at a company that made this move before she joined. She has never seen the room, because there is no room anymore, and she has spent three years hearing about regions and instances in meetings without a clear picture of what got replaced.

Then, in a roadmap review, an engineer with a spreadsheet says the thing that sounds so reasonable it is hard to argue with. "Our cloud bill is huge. If we bought our own servers and ran them ourselves, it would be a fraction of the cost. We should self-host."

The number on the spreadsheet is real. Bought outright and run at full tilt, the machines would cost less per hour than renting them. Everyone looks a little uncomfortable, because it sounds like the grown-up thing to do, the frugal thing, and nobody wants to be the person defending the expensive option.

Here is what Meera can say, once she knows what renting actually buys, and it does not require her to argue about the number, because the number is not wrong. It is incomplete.

"That per-hour number is real. What is not on the spreadsheet is what we would be buying back. Three things. First, the capital approval cycle: instead of asking for more capacity with a dropdown, we go back to the eighteen-month wait, the committee, and the guess about how big to build. Second, the lead time: when a customer needs us to be twice as big next quarter, we can no longer say yes on the call. Third, a maintenance contract and the people to run it, which is a permanent cost and a permanent hiring problem that has nothing to do with our product. So the question is not whether servers are cheaper per hour. It is whether we want to trade the freedom to be wrong about our size for a lower unit price."

The engineer, who is sharp and not wrong to raise it, pushes back. "But the bill really is huge. We cannot just ignore it."

And this is the second move, the one that separates a slogan from an actual grip on the problem. "We should not ignore it. But 'the bill is huge' is not yet a decision we can act on. The question is which part of the bill is huge, and why. Is it the capacity we keep running around the clock, which is the part that would genuinely be cheaper to own? Or is it the spiky part, the capacity we only need for the November rush, which is exactly the part that would be a nightmare to own? Those are opposite conclusions hiding inside the same big number. Let me get the bill broken down by which lines grow with our traffic and which ones just sit there, and then we can decide what, if anything, is worth owning."

That is the meeting changing. Not because Meera won an argument about dollars, but because she named the parts of the decision the spreadsheet left out, and then refused to treat one large number as if it were a single thing. The self-host number is a price. What it quietly cancels is a freedom. And the bill is never one number; it is a stack of decisions, and you cannot manage it until you pull them apart.

## Inside a real organization

It is easy, from the outside, to hear "we ran our own servers" as a line on an org chart. It was not. Owning the room was a standing commitment that touched more of the company than anyone upstairs realized until it was gone.

There were people whose whole week was capacity. Someone had to forecast how much the company would need in a year, translate that into racks and power and cooling, and be right enough that the business neither ran out nor drowned in idle machines. Someone had to keep the maintenance contract current and argue with the vendor when a drive failed. Someone had to walk the room, because a room full of computers has a smell when something is wrong, and an experienced person can hear a fan that is about to give out. There was a shelf of spare parts, because a failed drive at 2am is a very different problem if the replacement is in the building versus three days away by courier. None of this was the product. All of it was required for the product to exist.

The strangest part of the job was the forecasting, because it was a bet dressed as a spreadsheet. Once a year, someone had to predict how big the business would be, turn optimism into steel, and commit money to it long before the customers arrived to justify it. Guess high and you spent the year explaining idle machines to finance. Guess low and you spent it apologizing to customers and to the sales team whose growth you had quietly capped. The people who did this well were not the ones with the best spreadsheets. They were the ones who had been burned enough times to build in the right amount of fear. All of that skill, hard-won and genuinely valuable, thinned out the day the company could ask for more capacity and get it the same afternoon. A whole craft became a dropdown.

And then there were the nights.

Whichever way that night went, it puts a body in the room, and the body is the point. Renting from a landlord is not only a financial trade. It is also handing over the pager. When the cooling fails in a rented warehouse, the phone that rings belongs to a person you will never meet, employed by the landlord, whose entire job is that the room stays cold and the power stays on. The company that moved to the cloud gave up the room it could touch, and with it gave up the capacity forecast, the spare-parts shelf, the walk through the aisles, and the 2am call. For most companies, most of the time, that is the best part of the deal, and it never shows up on the spreadsheet either.

## The misconception

Everyone who has not run infrastructure carries a version of the same wrong belief, and it is worth naming plainly because it drives real decisions. The belief is that renting is throwing money away. That a serious company owns its things, and paying monthly forever is what you do when you are too small or too disorganized to buy.

For a house you will live in for thirty years, that instinct is correct. Rent that long and you will pay for the house twice and own nothing. But computing capacity in a growing business is not a house. It is closer to the fleet of vans a courier needs in December and not in February, or the extra staff a shop hires for the holidays. Nobody accuses a shop of throwing money away because it does not keep a full holiday workforce on payroll all year. The whole art is matching what you hold to what you need, and what you need keeps moving.

That is why the instinct runs backward here. Owning locks you to a guess you made eighteen months ago about a future you could not see. Renting lets the guess be wrong and cost you a month instead of a decade. You are not paying for the machine. You are paying for the right to give it back. Once you see the trade that way, half the arguments about the cloud stop being about money and start being about how certain you are, and how certain you are is the one thing a growing company never gets to be.

There is a companion belief, usually delivered as a put-down: that the cloud is "just someone else's computer." It is meant to sound wise, to puncture the hype, and at the most literal level it is true. Your data does sit on a computer that belongs to someone else. But it misses the entire point of what changed. A taxi is just someone else's car. A hotel is just someone else's bedroom. The whole value lives in the "someone else," the party who buys the thing, maintains it, replaces it when it breaks, and carries the cost of owning it on the nights you do not need it. Calling the cloud someone else's computer is like calling a hotel someone else's house. Technically correct, and exactly backward about why anyone would ever want one.

## Summary

Three things to keep from this chapter.

The gap is real and it is old: demand moves in weeks, physical capacity moves in years, and money cannot close the distance. Every growing business lives in that gap. The cloud is the thing that let companies rent their way across it instead of building across it.

Renting is not automatically cheaper, and pretending it is will lose you the argument. What renting buys is the freedom to be wrong about your size without it becoming a capital disaster. You are paying for the right to give the machine back.

And the room did not disappear when it went to the cloud. It moved into a warehouse you will never enter, and the pager moved to someone you will never meet. That move is the subject of everything that comes next, starting with a single ordinary act: uploading a photo, and following it all the way to where it lands.

## The Monday question

Next time someone proposes doing a thing "in-house to save money," whether it is servers, a tool, or a team, ask two things: what freedom would the company be giving back to get the lower price, and is that big number actually one thing, or a stack of different decisions wearing a single total?
