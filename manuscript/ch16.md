# Case study: connected vehicle telemetry

## The scenario

**Ardwick Commercial Vehicles** builds refrigerated delivery trucks and light
haulage vehicles. About 240,000 of its vehicles are on the road with telemetry
units fitted, and every vehicle built since 2019 ships with one as standard.

Each unit reports engine and drivetrain readings, refrigeration unit
temperature, location, fault codes, and driver behaviour metrics. Sampling is
every few seconds while the vehicle is running. Connectivity is mobile and
therefore intermittent: vehicles cross coverage gaps, park in underground bays,
and cross borders onto roaming agreements the finance team dislikes. Units
buffer locally and upload in bursts when a connection returns, so data arrives
late, out of order, and occasionally twice.

Today the data lands on a collection tier in Ardwick's own datacentre, which
writes it to a relational database sized for a fleet a fifth this size. The
database is nine terabytes and queries against it are measured in minutes. A
nightly job extracts a summary into a reporting system. Engineers who want raw
signal traces file a ticket and wait.

Three groups want things from the data. **Service centres** need to pull a
single vehicle's recent history while the customer is standing at the counter.
**Warranty and engineering** want to run analysis across the whole fleet and
several years — how a component behaves by climate, by duty cycle, by production
batch. **Product management** wants to predict refrigeration unit failures
before the load spoils, because a spoiled load costs more than the compressor.

Stated requirements:

- Ingest from the full fleet without the collection tier being a bottleneck, and
  without losing data buffered during connectivity gaps.
- Retain all raw telemetry. Nobody will commit to what future analysis needs, and
  discarded signal cannot be recovered.
- A service centre must retrieve one vehicle's recent history in under a second.
- Fleet-wide analysis over multiple years must be possible without asking an
  engineer to run an extract.
- Predict refrigeration failures with enough warning to schedule a service visit.
- Reduce what the business spends on the on-premises collection tier, which is
  due for hardware refresh.

## Working the problem

**Ingest.** Pub/Sub replaces the collection tier. It absorbs burst uploads
without provisioning, decouples the devices from everything downstream, and
removes the hardware refresh. Two properties of the scenario map straight onto
it. Delivery is at least once, and the units already send duplicates, so
processing must be idempotent or must deduplicate on a message identifier —
this is not a hypothetical, it is stated in the scenario. And arrival order is
not event order, because buffered data uploads late. Ordering keys would give
order within a vehicle at a throughput cost, but the better answer is to stop
relying on arrival order: each reading carries its own timestamp, and event time
is what the processing tier should use.

**Processing.** Dataflow, because this is precisely the workload Beam's model
exists for: unbounded data, event-time windowing, and late arrivals that must
land in the window they belong to rather than the window they showed up in. A
pipeline that windowed on processing time would attribute a truck's morning
readings to whenever it left the underground bay.

**Storage, and the split that carries this case study.** Raw telemetry goes to
Cloud Storage, cheaply and durably, with lifecycle rules moving older data to
colder classes. That satisfies "retain everything" without paying warehouse
rates for signal nobody has queried yet.

The service centre requirement — one vehicle, recent history, sub-second — is a
keyed lookup at volume, and that is Bigtable. Row key of vehicle identifier plus
a reversed timestamp, queried by prefix, returns the most recent readings
directly. Designing that row key *is* the schema design here.

Fleet-wide analysis over years is BigQuery: large scans, ad-hoc SQL, partitioned
by date and clustered on the columns engineers actually filter by — model,
batch, region. Partitioning and clustering are what keep the cost of "analyse
everything" defensible under on-demand pricing.

Three stores, three access patterns, and the exam tests exactly this split.
Bigtable cannot serve the ad-hoc analytical scan. BigQuery cannot serve a
sub-second keyed lookup at counter speed. Cloud Storage serves neither query but
is the only sane place for raw retention.

**The model.** Train in Vertex AI over historical data in BigQuery — this is
a large training set with engineered features, and feature consistency between
training and serving matters, which is what Feature Store addresses. Serving
splits by use: scoring the whole fleet nightly for scheduled maintenance is
batch prediction, and an always-on endpoint for it is wasted money. If a
prediction must be returned live to a service advisor, that path justifies an
online endpoint. BigQuery ML deserves consideration if the model stays simple
and the data never leaves the warehouse; Vertex AI earns its place when the
pipeline, registry, and lineage requirements appear.

## The tradeoff you defend

The tempting simplification is one store. Stream everything into BigQuery, serve
the service centre lookups from it, and be done — one system, one query
language, no synchronisation.

It fails twice. A point lookup against a warehouse does not reliably return in
under a second at this volume, and the counter requirement is a hard number. And
streaming all raw telemetry into warehouse storage pays a premium for years of
signal that may never be queried, when the requirement was only that it be
retained. The exam offers this answer often, because it looks like simplification
and reads as expensive over-ingestion once you check it against the two
requirements.

The mirror-image error is skipping Cloud Storage on the grounds that everything
useful is already in Bigtable and BigQuery. That discards the raw signal, and
the scenario says explicitly that nobody will commit to what future analysis
needs. Cheap durable capture of data with undecided value is the whole reason
object storage sits at the front of this design.

## What the exam would ask

Telemetry units buffer during connectivity gaps and upload in bursts. Which two
properties must the ingest and processing design accommodate, and which
windowing choice follows?

A service centre must retrieve one vehicle's recent history in under a second.
Which store, and what does the row key look like?

Engineering wants ad-hoc analysis across the fleet over four years. Which store,
and which two features control the cost of it?

Refrigeration failure predictions are used to schedule service visits. Which
prediction mode, and why is the alternative a cost error here?
