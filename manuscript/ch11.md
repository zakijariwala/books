# Reliability, observability, and recovery

The disaster recovery test happens quarterly. It has never completed inside its
window. Each time, the team gets further, documents the next blocker, and books
the following quarter. The runbook is accurate up to step nine. Somewhere in a
contract is an availability commitment, and when you ask where the number came
from, the answer is that it was in the previous contract.

Two failures are on display, and they are the ones section 6 of the exam guide
tests. A recovery capability that has never been proven is not a capability. And
a reliability target nobody derived is a number that cannot guide a single
design decision, because you cannot tell whether any given expenditure gets you
closer to it.

## SLIs, SLOs, and error budgets

A **service level indicator** is a measurement of something a user experiences:
the proportion of requests served successfully, the proportion served under some
latency, the freshness of data in a pipeline. CPU utilisation is not an
indicator, because no user has ever noticed it directly. Picking indicators that
track user experience rather than machine state is most of the skill.

A **service level objective** is the target for that indicator over a window.
It is an internal commitment, set below whatever you promise externally in an
SLA, so that missing the objective is a warning rather than a breach.

The **error budget** is what makes the objective operational. If the objective
is 99.9% over a month, the budget is the 0.1% you may spend. Spend it on
releases, experiments, and risk while it lasts; when it is exhausted, the
budget dictates that work stops and reliability work takes priority. That
mechanism connects reliability to release pace without an argument between
teams, which is why the exam likes it.

The case against 100% is economic, not technical. Each additional nine costs
disproportionately more, and past a point the user's own network and device
make your improvement invisible. Every scenario asking whether to add another
region is asking you to make that argument. The correct answer is the cheapest
design that meets the stated objective, not the most redundant design on the
list.

## Designing for failure

Availability comes in steps and each step costs.

A **zonal** deployment fails when the zone does. **Regional** deployment across
multiple zones survives a zone loss and is the baseline for production; regional
managed instance groups and regional clusters do this without a second copy of
your operational effort. **Multi-region** survives the loss of a region, and it
costs the most because it means data replication across distance, with the
consistency and latency consequences that carry.

The exam's discipline here is matching the topology to a stated requirement.
Multi-region is correct when the requirement says regional failure must be
survived, or when users are globally distributed and latency matters. It is a
distractor when the requirement says neither.

Autohealing on managed instance groups replaces instances failing an
application-level health check. The health check quality is the whole control:
one that verifies the process is listening will keep a fleet of deadlocked
applications in service.

**Graceful degradation** is worth stating explicitly because it appears as the
correct answer more often than candidates expect. Serving a cached catalogue
when the recommendation service is down, queueing writes when the downstream is
unavailable, disabling a non-essential feature under load. Partial service beats
an error page, and designs that fail entirely when one dependency fails are the
ones the exam is criticising.

## Disaster recovery tiers

Four tiers, and the choice follows two numbers.

**Backup and restore** keeps backups off-site and rebuilds when needed. Cheapest,
slowest, and entirely legitimate for workloads that can be down for a day.
**Cold standby** keeps data replicated and infrastructure defined but not
running. **Warm standby** keeps a scaled-down copy running and ready to take
traffic after scaling up. **Hot standby**, or active-active, runs full capacity
in both places and fails over with little or no interruption.

**RTO** is how long recovery may take. **RPO** is how much data you may lose.
Both come from the business, both drive the tier, and the tier drives the bill.
The exam's pattern is to state an RTO and RPO in the scenario and offer four
architectures; the correct one is the cheapest that meets both. An RPO near
zero forces synchronous or continuous replication. An RTO of minutes rules out
anything that rebuilds infrastructure from scratch, unless the infrastructure
is fully defined as code and the rebuild is genuinely automated.

Testing belongs in the design. An untested recovery plan has an unknown RTO,
which means the design does not meet a stated one. When a scenario mentions a
recovery capability that has never been exercised, that is the finding, and
scheduled testing is part of the answer.

## Observability

**Cloud Logging** routes logs through sinks to destinations: log buckets with
retention, Cloud Storage for cheap long-term retention, BigQuery for analysis,
Pub/Sub for onward processing. Exclusions drop high-volume low-value logs before
they are stored, which is the cost lever. Design the routing when you build the
platform, because after an incident is a bad time to discover the logs you need
were never retained.

Aggregated sinks at the organisation or folder level, exporting to a project
that the workload's administrators cannot modify, do double duty: analysis in
one place, and the audit integrity control from chapter 9.

**Log-based metrics** turn log patterns into time series you can alert on. This
is the specific answer to intermittent bursty failures — errors too infrequent
to notice in a dashboard and too frequent to ignore. Extract a metric from the
error pattern, alert on a threshold over a window, and the failure becomes
visible without anybody reading logs.

**Cloud Monitoring** carries dashboards, uptime checks against endpoints from
multiple locations, and alerting policies with notification channels. Alerting
on symptoms users feel rather than on causes is the practice worth carrying into
the exam, and it follows directly from the indicator discussion above.

**Cloud Trace** shows latency across a distributed request path, which is what
you need when a service is slow and no individual component looks slow.
**Cloud Profiler** attributes CPU and memory consumption inside a running
application. A scenario asking which service in a chain of twelve is adding
latency is describing tracing, not logging.

## Incident response

The exam's view of incidents is process-first, and it is consistent.

For a failure that has already happened, the correct next step is root cause
analysis, not re-architecture. A one per cent failure rate last week is
evidence, and the answer that investigates before rebuilding beats the answer
that rebuilds. Rebuilding without understanding fixes the symptom you guessed
at.

Postmortems are blameless by design, because a process that assigns blame
produces engineers who report less. The output is action items with owners, and
the exam favours answers producing those over answers producing a document.

Alert fatigue is a real finding. An alert that fires constantly and is routinely
dismissed has already stopped working, and reducing alert volume by alerting on
user-visible symptoms is a legitimate reliability improvement rather than
a reduction in vigilance.

## Recap

- Indicators measure what users experience; objectives are set below the SLA;
  error budgets convert reliability into a release-pace decision
- The correct topology is the cheapest one meeting the stated requirement.
  Multi-region without a stated need for it is a distractor
- RTO and RPO pick the DR tier. Near-zero RPO forces continuous replication;
  short RTO rules out rebuild-from-nothing
- An untested recovery plan has an unknown RTO and therefore fails any stated
  one
- Design log routing and retention at build time; log-based metrics catch
  intermittent failures; trace answers "which hop is slow"
