# Chapter 11: Cloud Economics, Why Your CFO is Crying

The CFO slid the printout across the table and let it sit there for a second before she spoke. "This is up forty percent since spring. Forty. Who approved that, and why am I finding out from the invoice?"

Nobody had approved it. That was the honest answer, and it was also the one nobody in the room wanted to give, because it sounds like negligence. The truth was gentler and stranger. No human being had decided to spend forty percent more. The number had grown the way a lawn grows, a little every day, out of a hundred small choices nobody had thought of as spending.

The finance team's first theory was the obvious one. The vendor raised prices. It is the theory everyone reaches for and it is almost always wrong; the landlords change their prices about as often as an electricity company reinvents the kilowatt, and usually downward. The bill went up because the company did something. It did the something in a dozen small changes over three months, and the invoice was the first place the pieces were added together.

That is the whole idea of this chapter, and it is worth stating before any of the mechanics. **The bill is a mirror.** It does not tell you what the cloud costs. It tells you what your product does. Every line on it is the shadow of a decision somebody made, usually for a good reason, usually without connecting the decision to the number it would grow.

## The business problem

Three things make this bill harder to manage than any other large line in a company's accounts.

**Nobody signs for it.** A capital purchase has an approver, a purchase order, and a person whose name is on it. Cloud spending is the accumulated result of engineers doing their jobs: creating a machine, keeping data, adding a feature. Each act is authorised in the sense that the person was employed to do it, and none is authorised in the sense finance means.

**It arrives after the fact.** You find out in arrears, weeks later, aggregated. The decision that grew the line was made in a sprint planning meeting nobody in finance attended, and by the time the invoice reveals it, three more sprints have happened.

**It is described in the vendor's vocabulary, not the company's.** The invoice is organised by product, region, and unit of consumption. It is not organised by feature, team, or customer, which are the only categories in which a business decision can be made. Somebody has to translate, and translation is the entire skill.

The translation problem is worth appreciating before dismissing it as clerical. An invoice can run to thousands of lines, arranged by things the landlord sells rather than things your company does, and no line says which feature caused it. Answering a question as simple as what one product area costs to run requires knowing which machines, shelves, and roads belong to it, and that mapping exists in the heads of engineers rather than in any document. This is why the honest answer to a CFO asking what a feature costs is often that nobody knows, and why that answer sounds evasive when it is merely true.

Which is why the meeting goes badly. Finance asks who approved it, engineering answers that everything on the list is necessary, and both are correct. The missing thing is a shared way to talk about which lines grow, why, and whether the growth was worth it.

## Four ways a line grows

Here is the translation, and it is the most useful thing in this chapter. Costs grow in four shapes, and naming the shape tells you what kind of problem you have.

**Some lines grow with users.** More customers, more activity, more requests, more machines from chapter four running to serve them. This is the healthy kind. If costs rose forty percent and usage rose forty percent, there is no problem in the room, only a CFO who has not been shown the second number. The correct response is to present the two together and move on.

**Some lines grow with data.** This is chapter five's ratchet, and it is the sneaky one. Data accumulates and is never deleted, so the line climbs whether or not the business does. Storage itself is modest per unit; the surprises come from movement, because taking data out is the metered direction. A new feature that ships a nightly copy to a partner, or lets customers download their history, or moves data between regions to serve distant users as chapter six described, converts a one-time decision into a permanent monthly line that grows with the archive rather than with the business.

**Some lines grow with mistakes.** The test environment nobody switched off. The capacity raised after an incident and never lowered, which chapter eight described. The machine kept at its largest size because reducing it carries a small risk and no reward. The service so chatty it makes a million small requests to do the work of a thousand. None of these are strategy. All of them are on the bill.

**And some lines grow with failure.** This one behaves unlike the others and catches companies at the worst moment. Retries when something is unavailable, duplicate work when a job is run twice, the extra capacity added during an incident, the data moved twice because a migration had to restart. Chapter nine's bad Tuesday arrives on the invoice five weeks later, and the finance team has no reason to connect the two. When a bill jumps in a month that contained an incident, that is the first place to look, and it is rarely the place anybody looks.

The reason to sort every line into one of these shapes is that they call for opposite responses. Growth with users should be celebrated and forecast. Growth with data should be governed with a retention decision, which chapter five recommended for cost and chapter ten recommended for containment, and which almost nobody has. Growth with mistakes should be cleaned up, and it is the only one of the four where the word "waste" is fair. Growth with failure should be traced back to the incident that caused it, which nobody thinks to do five weeks later.

It is worth saying plainly that only one of these four is a story about anyone doing anything wrong. Most cloud spending is a company operating. The reason the conversation so often becomes accusatory is that the invoice presents all four shapes in the same typeface, and a number that has gone up reads as a failure until somebody sorts it.

## One photograph, priced

The bill stops being abstract the moment you follow one object through it. So take the wedding photograph from chapter two and price its trip, not in money, but in which lines it touches.

**The road in.** Data arriving is welcomed at little or no charge. This line barely moves.

**The front door.** A share of a machine that is running anyway, for a fraction of a second. Small, and it grows with users.

**The warehouse.** The original photograph goes on a shelf and stays there. This is a tiny amount, charged every month, for a decade, and it never stops. The photograph will not be looked at again after the first week.

**The nine-second worker.** A machine exists briefly to make the preview and then stops. Charged by the piece. Grows with users, and stops entirely when they stop.

**The ledger.** A line is written recording that the photograph exists. Small, and it lands on the one component that cannot be copied, which is why the ledger tends to be the most expensive machine in the estate.

**The road out.** Every time anyone views that photograph, it leaves the building, and leaving is the metered direction. For a photograph viewed by six family members, negligible. For a photograph in a conversation of four hundred people who scroll past the preview every day, this becomes the largest line the picture generates, and it goes on generating it for years.

Now multiply. Four hundred thousand uploads in ninety seconds after a match, thirty days a month, several years of accumulated pictures nobody deletes.

Two things fall out of that arithmetic and they are the punchline of the chapter.

The first is that **the storage line and the movement line behave completely differently over time.** Storage rises with the total ever uploaded. Movement rises with how often things are looked at. A service whose users upload constantly and view rarely has one shape; a service whose users view constantly has another; and the same feature can be cheap at one and ruinous at the other.

The second is that **the expensive thing was not the photograph.** It was the decision to show a preview every time anyone scrolls. That is a product decision, made by somebody thinking about how the conversation should feel, and it set the largest recurring line the picture would ever generate. Nobody in that discussion was talking about money.

Which gives a rule worth carrying into any design discussion. **Ask what happens on the thousandth view, not the first.** Almost every cost in this book is trivial once and significant repeated, and the repetition is decided by product design rather than by infrastructure. A preview that loads on scroll, a page that refreshes on a timer, a notification that checks for updates every minute: each of those is a decision about how often something happens, and how often is the multiplier on every line beneath it.

That is also why cost conversations arrive too late to be cheap. By the time a line is large enough for finance to notice, the behaviour generating it has been shipped, adopted, and built upon, and changing it is a product change affecting customers rather than a setting somebody adjusts. The decision cost nothing to make and a great deal to unmake, which is the shape of nearly every expensive thing in this book.

## Two billing shapes worth knowing

Chapter four introduced machines rented by the hour. There is a second arrangement, and knowing which one a thing uses tells you how its line will behave.

**Per hour of machine.** You pay for the machine while it exists, busy or idle. This suits steady work. It is cheaper per unit of work when the machine is well used and wasteful when it is not, and it produces a bill with a floor: a minimum you pay every month regardless of what customers do.

**Per piece of work.** The nine-second worker. You pay for each unit performed and nothing between. This suits spiky, short work that holds nothing. It costs more per unit than a busy machine and produces a bill with almost no floor, which means a quiet month costs almost nothing and a viral month costs a great deal.

The second shape has a property that finance appreciates and engineering sometimes forgets: it is proportional. If the bill is per piece and the pieces are customer actions, then the cost of serving a customer is knowable, and a company that knows that number can price its product with confidence. The first shape hides that relationship inside a fixed monthly floor.

The committed-capacity trade from chapter four sits underneath both. Promise the landlord a year of steady use and the rate falls; keep the freedom to walk away and pay a premium for it. That premium is the price of the flexibility chapter one described, and most companies pay it on everything or commit to nothing, when the sensible arrangement is a base of committed capacity for what is always needed and flexible capacity for the peaks.

## The cost of leaving

Chapter three left a number unfinished, and this is where it belongs.

The exit cost of a landlord is three things added together, and companies usually count one.

**The work.** Everything written against that landlord's conveniences has to be rewritten. This is the part that gets estimated, and it gets estimated as though the team doing it will have nothing else to do.

**The freight.** Years of accumulated data have to move, in the metered direction, at a volume nobody has moved before, while the business keeps generating more. Chapter five's five weeks and an unexpected invoice.

**The knowledge.** The team is fluent in one landlord and will be slower for a year in another. This never appears in a migration plan and is frequently the largest of the three.

None of that is an argument against leaving. It is the number that makes the conversation honest, and the useful version of the question is not "are we locked in," which invites defensiveness, but **"if we had to leave within a year, what would we rewrite, what would we move, and who would we hire?"** That can be answered in an afternoon by people who are not on trial, and the answer is a figure a board can hold.

The reason to calculate it before anyone asks is that the number grows on its own, every month, as data accumulates and as more conveniences get adopted. It is the only line in this chapter that nobody bills you for and that everybody eventually pays.

There is a commercial use for the figure beyond deciding whether to leave, and it is the reason to have it in a drawer. A tenant who knows what leaving would cost negotiates differently from one who does not, and the landlords are aware of which kind of tenant they are speaking to. A company that can describe its exit credibly is in a conversation. A company that cannot is receiving terms.

The related judgement, and it is a real one rather than a trick, is how much portability to buy. Refusing every convenience to stay free costs the company speed every single day in exchange for an exit it may never take. Accepting every convenience buys speed now and raises the number quietly. The defensible position is usually in the middle and chosen on purpose: stay portable in the few places where switching is plausible, and take the conveniences everywhere else. What makes it a decision rather than a drift is that somebody wrote it down.

## What Meera does with this

Meera's job with the bill is translation, and it has three steps.

**Find the line that grew.** Not the largest line, the one that changed. Large lines are usually the business working. A line that moved is a decision that happened.

**Trace it to a decision.** Somebody built something, kept something, or forgot to switch something off. The trace is nearly always possible and rarely done, because it crosses the boundary between finance and engineering and belongs to neither.

**Ask whether the decision was worth it.** This is the only step that requires judgement, and it is hers. A search feature that lets customers dig through their whole history might triple a movement line and be the best money the company spends, because it is why customers renew. A logging setting might triple the same line and be worth nothing, because no human has read a log in a year. Same shape on the bill, opposite verdicts, and no number can distinguish them. Only the walk back to the decision can.

Her second contribution is preventive and worth more than any cleanup. When a feature is designed, she can ask: **does this grow with users, with data, or with neither?** A feature whose cost grows with the archive rather than with the customer base is a different financial animal from one that does not, and the cheapest moment to notice is before it is built. Nobody else in that room is going to ask.

Her third is to insist the bill be presented alongside a business number. A cloud bill on its own is a figure that only rises and therefore only alarms. The same figure per active customer, or per order, or per transaction, is a story about efficiency and can fall while the total climbs. Companies that never do this have a permanent argument about a number that is going up in a business that is also going up.

That single change does more for the quality of these conversations than any tooling. It converts the bill from a threat into a measure, and it gives engineering something to be proud of, since a falling cost per customer is an achievement that the raw total will never show. It also gives the board the only version of this figure that means anything, because a company whose infrastructure cost per customer is falling can grow with confidence and one whose figure is rising has a problem that growth will amplify rather than solve.

There is a fourth thing she can do, and it is the one that lasts longest after she moves on. She can ask for the bill to be split by the categories the company runs on: by feature, by team, or by customer segment. The invoice does not arrive that way, and arranging it requires somebody to decide how the estate maps onto the business. That work is unglamorous, takes a few weeks, and turns a monthly mystery into a monthly report. Companies that do it stop having the meeting from the opening of this chapter, because the answer to "who approved this" becomes visible on the day rather than eight weeks later.

## Inside a real organization

The pattern is that cost is everybody's concern and nobody's job.

Finance owns the total and cannot see what generates it. Engineering generates it and is measured on delivery and reliability, not on spend. No individual engineer's decision is large enough to matter, and the aggregate is a line item on the company's accounts. So the bill is discussed quarterly, in the abstract, by people who cannot act on it, and acted on annually in a panic, by people who were not consulted.

The second pattern is that the panic works and does not last. A cost-reduction effort finds the obvious waste, removes it, and produces a satisfying fall. Nothing changes about how decisions get made, so the line begins climbing again the following quarter, and the exercise repeats about a year later. Companies that break the cycle do one unglamorous thing: they make the cost of a change visible to the people making it, at the moment they make it, rather than to finance eight weeks later.

The third is that the largest savings are usually decisions rather than optimisations. Turning things off, keeping less, and moving data less often are all decisions that require somebody with authority to accept a small risk. Chapter five named the reason this stalls: the saving belongs to the company and the risk belongs to a person. No amount of tooling fixes an incentive.

The fourth is that the AI machines from chapter four have changed the shape of this conversation since around 2023. They are expensive relative to ordinary compute and punish idleness harder than anything else on the invoice, which means a line that used to grow slowly can now grow steeply from a single project. A company that has not had this conversation in the last year is working from an old picture of its own bill.

Two things about that line make it behave unlike the rest. It can be created by a small number of people in a short time, so the usual comfort that no individual decision is large enough to matter no longer holds. And the work is often exploratory, which means the thing generating the cost has no revenue attached to it yet and no obvious moment when somebody decides it has finished. An experiment that nobody formally ended is the most expensive version of the test environment from the opening of this chapter.

The fifth pattern is about who ends up owning this. Companies reach a size where somebody is made responsible for cloud cost, and the appointment tends to go to a technical person, on the reasonable grounds that the subject looks technical. The role fails in that shape, because the decisions that move the number are product decisions and the person holding it has no standing in those rooms. It works when the owner is somebody who can sit in a design discussion and ask what happens on the thousandth view, which is a business skill applied to a technical subject rather than the other way round.

## The misconception

The belief is that the cloud got expensive.

People say it the way they say the weather turned cold, as though a cost were a season that happens to you. It did not get expensive. Your product changed, and the bill, being an honest mirror, reflected the change. That is uncomfortable, because it means the number is not the vendor's fault or the market's fault. It is a record of your own decisions, most of which were reasonable, none of which were counted.

The sharper correction is about what the bill is for. **A cloud bill is the most detailed description of your product's behaviour that anyone will ever write, and it is written by an outside party with no incentive to flatter you.** Read that way it is not a demand for payment. It is a report, arriving monthly, saying exactly what your company did.

Which suggests a different response to the forty percent. Not "who approved this," which has no answer, but "which line grew, what decision grew it, and would we make that decision again knowing the number." That is a meeting a CFO can sit in without reaching for blame and an engineer can sit in without feeling accused, because it treats the bill as what it is: not a price that was done to you, but a mirror you finally turned around.

The forty percent in the opening was three things. Two were customers doing more of what customers are supposed to do, and the company was glad to pay for those. The third was a test environment created for a launch in the spring, still running, serving nobody. It took an afternoon to find. Once it was named, the decision took ten minutes.

## Summary

Three things to keep.

Lines grow in four shapes: with users, with data, with mistakes, and with failure. Naming the shape tells you whether to celebrate, to govern, to clean up, or to look back at an incident, and the four call for opposite responses.

One customer action touches every layer, and the largest recurring cost it generates is usually set by a product decision made by somebody who was not discussing money. Storing rises with everything you have ever held; moving rises with how often anyone looks.

And the bill is a mirror, not a price. The useful question is never why it is large but which line changed, what decision changed it, and whether the company would make that decision again knowing the number.
