# Chapter 9: Reliability, The Day the Internet Broke

At twenty past nine on a Tuesday, a region belonging to one of the large landlords began having a bad morning, and several thousand companies discovered at the same moment that they were tenants.

Meera's product was one of them. Nothing her company owned was broken. No release had gone out, no server had failed, nobody had touched anything. The application was intact and could not be reached, and there was nothing whatsoever for her engineers to do about it except refresh a status page that said, for the first forty minutes, that the landlord was investigating.

By ten o'clock she was writing the apology email, which is the job that falls to whoever owns the customer relationship when the thing that failed belongs to somebody else. She wrote that the company was experiencing a disruption caused by an infrastructure provider, that its teams were monitoring, and that it would update customers as it learned more. Every sentence was true and every sentence was useless, and she knew while writing it that the customers reading it did not care whose warehouse had flooded.

Then somebody in the meeting said the reassuring thing, which is what this chapter is about. "At least we have an SLA."

They did. It entitled them, once they had noticed, applied, and been assessed, to a credit against the following month's bill, proportional to the fraction of the month that had been lost. That credit came to a small figure. The four largest customers spent the afternoon asking whether this would happen again, one of them mentioned their renewal, and none of that appeared anywhere in the guarantee.

## The business problem

Every company that runs on somebody else's infrastructure is exposed to three things it does not control, and confuses them at its cost.

**What breaks**, which is now mostly not yours. The trip from chapter two crosses several businesses; you operate a couple of the stops and rent the rest. Your uptime is the product of everybody's uptime, which is a fact of arithmetic and not a criticism of anyone.

**What the contract pays**, which is far less than what the failure costs, and is never intended otherwise.

**What the customer holds you responsible for**, which is all of it. No customer in history has accepted that a supplier's supplier is not the supplier's problem. They bought from you.

The gap between the second and the third is where the apology email lives, and it is why reading the guarantee before signing it is a business skill rather than a legal formality.

The size of that gap is worth stating plainly, because it is the thing people are most surprised by. A morning of unavailability costs a company lost transactions, the staff time of everyone who dropped their work to respond, the customers who quietly decide to evaluate a competitor, and whatever the renewal conversation now contains. The credit returns a slice of one month's infrastructure bill. Those two figures are not close, they are not intended to be close, and no amount of negotiation brings them close, because a vendor cannot underwrite the businesses of every tenant in the building.

Which means the guarantee is best read not as protection but as information. It tells you how much unavailability the vendor considers normal, which is the number you should be planning around. Read that way, an afternoon spent with four contracts produces a realistic picture of what your product can promise, and that picture is worth having before somebody in a sales meeting invents a better one.

## Failure is not a switch

The word "outage" suggests a state: the system is up, or it is down, and a graph somewhere goes flat. That describes the rarest and easiest kind of failure.

Most real failures are partial, and the partial ones are worse, because nothing announces them.

**Some things work and others do not.** The six stops from chapter two fail one at a time. The warehouse is fine and the ledger is unreachable, so customers can browse and not buy. The ledger is fine and the warehouse is slow, so the page loads and every image is a grey rectangle. From the inside, most dashboards are green, because most components are healthy. From the outside, the product is broken.

**Some customers are affected and others are not.** A failure confined to one region, one zone, or one slice of traffic produces a support queue in which half the reporters are describing an emergency and half the company's own staff cannot reproduce it. Chapter six's distant customer problem and chapter seven's stale read are both this shape, and so is a large share of everything else.

**Some things are slow rather than dead**, which is the hardest of all. A component that fails cleanly gets detected, removed, and replaced by an automatic process that was designed for exactly that. A component that answers in eight seconds instead of eighty milliseconds passes every check that asks whether it is alive, keeps receiving work, and holds up everything waiting behind it. Systems handle death well and sickness badly, which is true of most systems, including hospitals.

**And some things half-succeed.** The upload from chapter two is the standing example: the file lands on the shelf and the line in the ledger never gets written, so there is a photograph nobody can find and nobody can delete, being paid for every month. Nothing errored. Each individual box did what it was asked. The failure exists only in the relationship between two boxes, which is precisely where nothing is monitored.

That last category is worth holding onto, because it is the one that produces the customer complaint no engineer can reproduce and the storage line nobody can account for. A supply chain, as chapter two argued, can half-work. This is what half-working looks like a month later.

There is a reason these four categories are hard to catch, and it is the same reason in each case. Monitoring is arranged around components, because components are what a team owns and what a chart can show. Every category above is a failure of the relationship between components, or of the experience of a particular customer, and neither of those belongs to anyone. The company can therefore be entirely green and entirely broken, which is not negligence but a consequence of measuring the things that are easy to measure.

The practical version of this for a business reader is a single sentence worth saying in a meeting after any incident. **Was anything left in an inconsistent state?** Not was it fixed, not is it up, but did anything half-happen while the trouble was going on. The answer is often yes and is often discovered weeks later by a customer, and the window to find it cheaply is the day of the incident when everybody is already looking.

## What the guarantee says

An SLA is a service level agreement, and reading one is a fifteen-minute job that almost nobody does before signing.

Four things are worth knowing, and none require legal training.

**It is a refund policy, not insurance.** If the service misses its target, you may claim a credit against future spending with the same vendor. The credit is calculated from what you paid them, never from what the failure cost you. A morning that costs you four customer relationships and a renewal conversation returns a fraction of one month's bill, to be spent with the company that had the bad morning. That is not a scandal, it is what the document says, and it is the correct thing to plan around rather than complain about.

**You usually have to claim it.** Credits are not applied because an outage happened. Somebody at your company has to notice, gather the evidence, and submit a claim within a stated window. If nobody owns that, the entitlement expires unexercised, which is the normal outcome.

**The target is a time budget, and the budget is larger than it sounds.** A guarantee expressed in nines is a promise about a percentage of a period, and the useful move is to convert it into minutes. A little over ninety-nine point nine percent of a month leaves around forty minutes of permitted unavailability. Ninety-nine point nine nine leaves about four. Both numbers look like certainty on a slide and neither is; they are allowances, and a vendor that uses its full allowance every month has met its obligation in full while giving you an outage every month.

**The exclusions are where the document lives.** Scheduled maintenance is usually excluded. Anything caused by your own configuration is excluded, which is chapter three's shared responsibility line appearing in the contract. Failures of components you are using but which sit outside the specific covered service are excluded. And the measurement is generally of whether the vendor's service was available, not whether your customers could use your product, which are different questions with different answers.

The last of those is the one to hold. **The guarantee measures their component, and your customer measures your product.** Those two measurements can disagree for an entire morning, and the contract is about the first one.

The arithmetic of nines is worth converting once and remembering, because a percentage on a slide is designed to feel like a promise and the same figure in minutes feels like what it is. Somewhere above ninety-nine point nine percent, a month contains around forty minutes of permitted absence. Add a nine and it falls to roughly four. Take one away and it rises past seven hours. Each nine is therefore not an increment; it is a tenfold change in what you are entitled to complain about, and the difference between two adjacent figures on a vendor comparison table is the difference between an afternoon and a coffee break.

The other reason to convert is that it makes the guarantee comparable to your own promises. If your company has told customers it is available at a certain standard, and the four suppliers underneath it each permit themselves forty minutes, the sums stop being abstract. There is no arrangement of four suppliers each allowed forty minutes that reliably produces a product allowed forty minutes, and seeing that written down has ended more optimistic commitments than any argument.

## What this means for what you promise

Which produces a question a business reader should ask before her own company promises anything.

Your product's realistic availability is bounded by everything underneath it. If the trip in chapter two crosses six stops and you rent four of them, your ceiling is the combination of all four suppliers' behaviour plus your own, and combinations are less reliable than their parts. Three suppliers each achieving a good number produce a worse number together, because their bad moments do not overlap politely.

So a company promising its customers a higher standard than the sum of its suppliers is writing a cheque against somebody else's account. This happens often, because the promise is made by a commercial team reading a competitor's website and the arithmetic is known to an infrastructure team nobody asked.

The way out is not to buy better suppliers. It is to decide, deliberately, which failures the company will engineer around and which it will accept. Running in two regions protects against one region's bad morning and costs everything chapter six described. Accepting the risk costs nothing until the Tuesday it costs a great deal. Both are legitimate. Only one of them is usually a decision.

Chapter three's availability zones are the cheap version of this and worth understanding as a distinct choice. Spreading across several buildings inside one region protects against one building's power, cooling, or flood, costs little, and is what most companies should do without much debate. It does not protect against the region itself having a bad morning, which is what happened on that Tuesday, and confusing the two is common. A company can be spread across three zones, believe itself protected, and still be entirely unavailable when the region has a problem, because all three buildings sit in the same city under the same regional systems.

The expensive version, a second region, protects against the Tuesday and brings a burden the company will carry every day for the sake of an event that may not arrive this decade: two of everything to keep in step, data moving between them at the metered rate from chapter five, and the awkward question from chapter seven of which copy decides what is true. It is the right choice for some businesses and a poor one for many, and the correct way to reach it is by asking what an hour of unavailability costs the business, then comparing that against the standing price of the insurance.

Most companies have never calculated the first number, which makes the second impossible to judge. So the decision defaults to inertia, and the choice is reopened the morning after an outage, when the cost of an hour has been demonstrated and nobody is in a state to weigh it fairly.

## What Meera does with this

Meera's first move is to read her own vendors' guarantees, which will take an afternoon, and to write down for each one: what is the time budget, what is excluded, who at this company would claim the credit.

Her second is to ask the question that reframes the tense meeting: **"What is our realistic availability, given everything we depend on?"** That number is knowable and it is usually lower than what the sales team has been implying. Discovering the gap in a planning meeting is a great deal cheaper than discovering it in a contract negotiation with a customer who kept the marketing page.

Her third belongs to the moment after an incident, and it is where she can do the most good. The instinct after an outage is to prevent that specific failure from recurring, which produces a list of narrow fixes for a thing that will not happen the same way twice. The more useful question is: **"What did our customers see, and could we have shown them something better?"** During the forty minutes when nothing could be done, the product could have shown a clear message rather than a browser error, the status page could have said something human, and the four large customers could have been telephoned before they emailed. None of that is engineering, all of it changes what the outage cost, and all of it is decided in advance, which is chapter eight's lesson wearing different clothes.

There is a preparatory version of the third question, and it costs an hour rather than an incident. Take the list of everything the product depends on, and for each one write a sentence describing what a customer experiences when it is unavailable. Most companies discover two things doing this. A few dependencies would stop the business, and those deserve real engineering attention. Most would degrade something nobody would notice, provided somebody has decided in advance not to wait for them. The list separates the two, and until it exists every supplier is being treated as equally critical, which means none of them is.

And her fourth is about the apology itself, since she is the one writing it. The email she sent said the company was experiencing a disruption caused by an infrastructure provider. Customers read that as an excuse, because it is one. What they want is what happened, what it meant for them specifically, what has been done, and what changes. A company that can say "your data was never at risk, no orders were lost, the eleven that failed have been resubmitted, and here is what we are changing" has converted an outage into evidence of competence. That message requires knowing which of the six stops failed and what half-succeeded, which is what the last seven chapters were for.

## Inside a real organization

The pattern that surprises people is that reliability work is invisible when it succeeds and unrewarded when it prevents something.

Nobody is thanked for the outage that did not happen. There is no meeting about the Tuesday that was ordinary. So the engineering that produces reliability competes for time against features that are visible, and it loses, until an incident makes it briefly the only priority in the company. Reliability therefore arrives in bursts following failures rather than steadily, and the burst is aimed at whatever failed last, which is rarely what will fail next.

The second pattern is that companies measure the wrong availability. The dashboards report whether components responded, which is the vendor's question. Almost nobody measures whether a customer could complete the thing they came to do, which is the only question that matters and the only one that would have caught chapter six's Singapore problem, chapter seven's stale reads, and the half-succeeded uploads sitting in the warehouse. A single measurement of whether real customers are succeeding at the main task is worth more than a wall of component charts, and it is rarer than it should be because it belongs to no team.

The third is that dependencies accumulate quietly, exactly like the data in chapter five and the round trips in chapter six. Every convenient service a team adopts is another party whose bad morning becomes yours, and the decision to adopt it is made by whoever needed it that week. Few companies can list what they depend on. Fewer have asked what happens to the product when each one is unavailable, and the answer for a surprising number is that the whole product stops for the sake of a component doing something peripheral.

That last case deserves its own attention, because it is both common and cheap to fix. A product calls out to something small: an address lookup, an analytics collector, a support widget, a service that decides which of two designs to show. The provider has a bad morning. If the product waits for an answer that is never coming, the page does not load, and a company is now unavailable because a peripheral supplier is. The customer cannot buy anything, and the thing that failed was decorative.

The fix is a decision rather than a technology: for each outside thing the product calls, somebody says what happens when it does not answer. Wait, or continue without it. Almost always the answer should be continue, and almost always nobody has been asked. This is chapter eight's ranked list of what may be sacrificed, applied to suppliers instead of features, and it is the single highest-value hour a product manager can spend on reliability.

The fourth pattern is what happens to the report afterwards. A serious incident produces a document, and the document is usually written to survive being read by a customer, which makes it a statement rather than an inquiry. The version that improves anything is blunter, names what nobody knew, and is read by people who were not there. Companies that keep those two documents separate learn something. Companies that write only the first learn to write well.

## The misconception

The belief is that the cloud is more reliable than what came before, and therefore that reliability is now somebody else's problem.

The first half is true. The landlord's building has better power, better cooling, better physical security, and more redundancy than the room off the ring road in chapter one, and it is maintained by people whose entire job it is. Individual components fail far less often than they used to.

The second half does not follow, and the reason is that you now have more of them, run by more parties, connected across more distance. Reliability moved from being a property of one room you controlled to being a property of a supply chain you assembled. The individual links improved. The number of links grew. Whether the product is more reliable than it used to be depends on which of those moved further, and that is a question about your particular arrangement rather than about the cloud.

The sharper correction is about the guarantee. **An SLA is not a promise that a service will not fail. It is a statement of how much failure is contractually normal, and what you get back when that is exceeded.** Read that way it is a useful document. Read as a safety net, it produces the meeting in which somebody says "at least we have an SLA" and everyone feels better for no good reason.

For a reader with no contracts to read, the same shape shows up whenever a familiar service is unavailable and a large part of the internet appears to go with it. Those mornings are not evidence that the internet is fragile. They are evidence that a great many separate companies rented from the same landlord, in the same region, and that nobody outside those companies had any way of knowing they had done so.

That is the uncomfortable finding underneath this whole chapter, and it has nothing to do with any vendor's competence. Concentration is a consequence of the economics in chapter one: a small number of landlords can build at a scale nobody else can match, so a small number of landlords host an enormous share of everything. Each individual building is far better run than the room off the ring road ever was. The correlated risk is larger, because a bad morning is no longer one company's problem but a shared one, and the companies sharing it did not know they were doing so until the morning arrived.

## Summary

Three things to keep.

Failure is rarely a switch. Things half-work, some customers are affected and others are not, and the hardest failure is slowness rather than death, because systems detect death and keep feeding work to the sick. The half-succeeded write is the standing example: nothing errored, and there is a file nobody can find that you pay for monthly.

A guarantee is a refund policy, not insurance. It pays a fraction of what you paid the vendor, you generally have to claim it, its target is a time budget in minutes rather than a promise, and its exclusions are the document. It measures their component, not your customer's experience.

And your realistic availability is bounded by everything you depend on. Promising customers more than your suppliers give you is writing a cheque against somebody else's account, and the arithmetic is known to a team nobody asked.

## The Monday question

Find the guarantee for the vendor your product most depends on, convert its target into minutes per month, and ask who at your company would file the claim. If nobody would, you are carrying the risk without collecting the refund, which is the worst available arrangement and a common one.
