# Security and compliance

The auditor arrives with a spreadsheet. Column one lists controls, column two
asks who attests to them, and column three is empty because nobody has filled it
in since the last audit. Somewhere in the contract with your largest customer
is a clause saying their data does not leave the country. In a rack downstairs
is an HSM that was bought for a project that got cancelled, and the key
ceremony documentation for it is a Word file with three authors.

Security questions carry 17.5% of the exam, and they are rarely about
firewalls. They are about who holds keys, what stops an authorised person
copying data out, and how you prove any of it afterwards.

## Encryption

Everything at rest is encrypted by default, with Google managing the keys. This
is true, it is free, and it answers fewer exam questions than candidates expect,
because compliance requirements are rarely about whether data is encrypted.
They are about who controls the key and who can be compelled to produce it.

**CMEK**, customer-managed encryption keys, means the key lives in Cloud KMS in
your project, you control its rotation and its lifecycle, and you can disable or
destroy it. Destroying the key destroys access to the data encrypted under it,
which is both the control being bought and the risk being taken on. CMEK is the
answer to "we must control the keys", "we must be able to revoke access to the
data cryptographically", and "key rotation must follow our schedule".

**CSEK**, customer-supplied keys, means you pass the raw key material with each
request and Google never stores it. It applies to a narrow set of services and
is largely superseded by CMEK for new designs.

**Cloud HSM** is Cloud KMS backed by FIPS-validated hardware security modules,
for requirements that name a hardware protection level. **Cloud External Key
Manager** keeps the key in a supported external key management system outside
Google entirely; Google calls out to it and never holds the key material. That
is the answer when a clause says keys must never reside in the cloud provider's
systems, and it brings an availability dependency with it, because losing
reachability to the external manager loses access to the data.

Separation of duties on key administration is the pattern that ties this
together. The person who can administer a key should not be the person who can
read the data it protects. Splitting the key project from the data project, with
different administrators, is a standard design and a common correct answer.

## VPC Service Controls

IAM answers who may call an API. It does not answer where the data may go
afterwards. A user with legitimate read access to a Cloud Storage bucket can
copy its contents to a personal project, and no IAM policy on your project
prevents it, because every action in that sequence was authorised.

**VPC Service Controls** draws a **service perimeter** around projects and the
Google-managed services they use. Data inside the perimeter cannot be copied to
resources outside it, even by a principal with permissions on both sides. This
is an exfiltration control, and it operates at a different layer from firewall
rules, which govern network reachability rather than API-level data movement.

Perimeters need deliberate holes. **Ingress and egress rules** allow specified
identities and services to cross in defined directions. **Access levels**
qualify access by attributes such as source IP range, device state, or
geography. And **dry-run mode** is the operational answer that appears in
questions about deploying a perimeter without breaking production: it logs what
would have been denied so you can find the legitimate flows before enforcing.

The canonical design is a perimeter around the projects holding regulated data,
with narrow egress rules for the pipelines that must legitimately move data out,
and everything else denied. Expect a scenario shaped like a healthcare records
platform to be asking for exactly this.

## Access control beyond IAM

**Context-aware access** conditions access on attributes of the request rather
than only the identity making it: corporate device, managed and compliant OS,
known network, geography. It is how a requirement like "contractors may access
this only from managed devices" becomes a control rather than a policy
document.

**Identity-Aware Proxy** puts identity-based access control in front of an
application, so authorisation happens at the proxy rather than inside the app
and the app needs no public exposure. It replaces the VPN-for-internal-apps
pattern, which is what a scenario is describing when it complains about
maintaining VPN clients for access to internal web tools.

**Secret Manager** stores credentials, API keys, and certificates with
versioning, IAM control, and audit logging. What belongs in it is anything you
would otherwise put in an environment variable, a config file, or a repository.
What does not belong in it is a Google service account key, because the correct
answer there is to not have one: use Workload Identity or impersonation, as in
chapters 2 and 5.

## Detection and audit

**Cloud Audit Logs** come in kinds and they behave differently. Admin Activity
logs record configuration changes, are always on, and cannot be disabled. Data
Access logs record reads and writes of data, are mostly off by default, and cost
money at volume once enabled. That asymmetry is examinable: a scenario asking
who read a sensitive table needs Data Access logging turned on in advance, and
the answer that assumes it was already there is wrong.

**Security Command Center** aggregates findings, misconfigurations, and threats
across the organisation. Tiers differ in what detection is included, so verify
current tier contents rather than memorising them.

Exporting logs to a sink in a **separate project**, with tight permissions and
retention or bucket lock applied, is the integrity control. A project
administrator who can delete their own logs cannot be audited. When a question
asks how to guarantee logs survive an insider with administrative rights, the
answer moves the logs out of that person's control.

## Compliance

Data residency is handled by **organization policy constraints** that restrict
which regions resources may be created in, combined with choosing regional
rather than multi-region resources. It is a preventive control applied at the
hierarchy level from chapter 2, not something enforced per project by review.

**Assured Workloads** builds on this for regulatory regimes, applying a package
of controls covering location, personnel access, and supported services against
a named compliance programme. When a scenario names a specific regime and asks
for a supported control set rather than a hand-built one, that is the offering.

Shared responsibility, stated plainly: Google secures the infrastructure and the
managed services. You are responsible for identity, permissions, configuration,
and your data. The dividing line moves with the service, which is why serverless
options carry less of your responsibility than virtual machines. Exam answers
that assume Google handles your IAM misconfiguration are wrong by construction.

## Exam traps

"Data must not leave the region" is organization policy plus regional
resources. It is not a firewall rule, and it is not a perimeter, though a
perimeter may also be required for a different sentence in the same scenario.

"We must hold the keys" is CMEK. "Keys must never exist in the provider's
systems" is External Key Manager. "Keys must be held in validated hardware" is
Cloud HSM. Three similar sentences, three different products.

Exfiltration by an authorised user is VPC Service Controls. Any answer that
tightens IAM has misread the threat, because the actor already has the
permission.

An audit question about who read data depends on Data Access logs having been
enabled beforehand. Admin Activity logs will not tell you.

Answers that store a service account key securely are still worse than answers
that eliminate the key.

## Recap

- Default encryption is free and rarely the answer; the question is who
  controls the key. CMEK, Cloud HSM, and External Key Manager map to three
  distinct compliance sentences
- Separate key administration from data access, in different projects with
  different administrators
- VPC Service Controls stop authorised principals moving data out. IAM cannot.
  Deploy perimeters in dry-run first
- Identity-Aware Proxy and context-aware access replace VPN-shaped answers for
  internal application access
- Admin Activity logs are always on; Data Access logs are not. Export logs to a
  project the subject cannot administer
