# Migration

Four hundred virtual machines. The configuration management database says three
hundred and sixty, and both numbers are wrong. Nobody can tell you what half of
them talk to, because the people who could have moved on and the documentation
describes an architecture that was decommissioned twice. Two applications are
supported by vendors who have opinions about where they run. The datacentre
lease expires in eleven months and the site owner has already found the next
tenant.

That last sentence decides more of the architecture than the other five put
together, and recognising it is the skill this chapter teaches. Google's own
learning path has no migration course, yet every official case study is a
migration or modernisation scenario. This is where the exam and the training
material diverge most, so it is where preparation pays best.

## The strategies

**Rehost** moves the workload as it is. Same operating system, same
application, different hypervisor underneath. Fastest, lowest risk, and it
banks none of the cloud's operational benefits. **Replatform** makes targeted
changes on the way: the self-managed MySQL becomes Cloud SQL, the file share
becomes Cloud Storage, the application itself is untouched. **Refactor**
rewrites the application for the target, which is where containers, managed
services, and event-driven designs enter. **Repurchase** replaces it with a
SaaS product. **Retire** switches it off, and on most estates a real fraction of
the inventory qualifies. **Retain** leaves it where it is, at least for now.

The taxonomy is easy and the exam does not test it directly. It tests
selection. Three constraints decide most real migrations, and all three appear
in scenarios:

**Time.** A fixed exit date compresses everything. If the lease ends in eleven
months, you rehost the bulk and modernise afterwards from inside the cloud,
because a refactor programme cannot be scheduled against someone else's
calendar. An answer that proposes rewriting forty applications before an
immovable date is wrong on delivery grounds, not technical ones.

**Change tolerance.** Vendor-supported software with a support matrix, or an
application whose source no longer builds, cannot be refactored. The scenario
usually says so in one sentence buried in the middle.

**Team capability.** A team that has never run Kubernetes will not operate a
refactored microservice estate well, and training is a dependency with a
duration. Capability is a legitimate architectural input, and the exam rewards
answers that account for it.

Cost sits underneath all three but rarely decides alone. Rehosting an
over-provisioned estate one-for-one carries the waste across; that is a real
finding, and the standard response is rightsizing during the move rather than
abandoning the strategy.

## Discovery and assessment

You cannot plan waves against an inventory you do not have. Discovery collects
what is running, how big it actually is against how big it was provisioned,
and — the hard part — what talks to what.

**Migration Center** is the assessment tooling: it inventories the estate,
gathers utilisation, sizes the target, and produces cost estimates. Its output
matters most for rightsizing, because on-premises machines are habitually sized
for a peak that never arrives.

Dependency discovery is the part that gets skipped and the part that sinks wave
plans. Three sources, none sufficient alone. The configuration management
database tells you what somebody once believed. Network flow data tells you what
actually talks to what, which is the only evidence that survives contact with a
cutover. And the people who operate the systems tell you about the annual
process that runs in March and appears in neither of the first two. Budget real
time for this; an inventory assembled in a fortnight is a list, not a dependency
map.

Watch for the dependencies that do not appear as traffic: a shared filesystem
four applications mount, a licence server, a scheduler that triggers jobs across
six systems, a hard-coded IP address in a config file written in 2014.

## Wave planning

Plan waves around **dependency clusters**, not around teams or around
convenience. Applications that talk to each other constantly should move
together or accept the latency of talking across the hybrid link during the
transition. A wave that splits a chatty pair leaves you paying for Interconnect
capacity and latency for months.

A wave plan that holds up under questioning has four properties:

- **Wave zero is not a workload.** It is the landing zone: hierarchy, networking,
  identity federation, the hybrid link, logging. Nothing else can move until
  this exists, and it is the wave most often left out of a plan.
- **Wave one is deliberately boring.** Something low-risk with a forgiving
  audience, chosen so the process gets debugged on a system nobody will lose
  sleep over. Teams that start with the crown jewels learn their runbook is
  wrong at the worst possible moment.
- **Waves are grouped by dependency, ordered by risk.** Within that, move whole
  clusters so the hybrid boundary falls where traffic is thin.
- **The last wave is not the last date.** Leave slack between the final wave and
  any immovable deadline. Migrations discover work; a plan with no float has
  already failed, it just does not know yet.

Each wave needs its own cutover window, rollback criteria, and verification
steps. Reusing the previous wave's runbook without revisiting them is how
wave three fails after two clean ones.

## Parallel run and cutover

The cutover question is the same in every migration: how much downtime does the
business accept, and how do you know the new side is right?

**Parallel run** answers the second. Both systems run, both process the same
input, and you compare outputs until the difference is either zero or explained.
It is the strongest verification available and it is expensive — double
infrastructure, double operational load, and a reconciliation process somebody
has to staff. Use it where being wrong is worse than being late: financial
calculation, billing, regulatory reporting.

Its failure mode is that it never ends. Nobody wants to be the person who
switched off the old system, so a three-month parallel run becomes a year of
paying twice. **Decide the exit criteria and the end date before it starts**, and
make ending it someone's named responsibility.

Where parallel run is too expensive, the alternatives are a **pilot** — move a
subset of users or one region first — or **continuous replication with a short
cutover window**, which is what the tooling below is for. Ranking them by
confidence bought against cost paid is exactly the judgement the exam asks for.

## Rollback planning

Every cutover plan needs an answer to "what if it goes wrong", agreed in writing
while everyone is calm.

Three things must be decided in advance. **The criteria**: the specific,
measurable conditions that trigger a revert, not a judgement call made at 03:00
by tired people. **The deadline**: the latest moment at which reverting is still
possible, which is usually the point where the old system's data has diverged
too far to reconcile. **The authority**: one named person who can call it, not a
committee assembled on a bridge line.

The uncomfortable truth is that rollback gets harder the longer you wait, and
the pressure to persevere grows over exactly the same period. That is why the
decision point is a scheduled moment rather than a feeling.

## Moving the workloads

**Migrate to Virtual Machines** replicates on-premises VMs into Compute Engine
with the source still running, then cuts over. The replication happening while
the source stays live is the point: it keeps the outage window to a cutover
rather than a copy.

**Migrate to Containers** takes suitable VM workloads and produces container
artefacts for GKE, which is the assisted path for replatforming rather than
rehosting.

**Database Migration Service** handles managed database migrations with
continuous replication, so the cutover is a switch rather than an export and
import. The related tooling handles heterogeneous moves where the engine
changes, which is a bigger project than the same-engine case and should be
sized as one.

The cutover question is the same in every case: how much downtime does the
business accept, and what is the rollback if the new side misbehaves. Continuous
replication buys a short window. A dual-run period, where both sides run and
reconcile, buys confidence and costs double for its duration. Decide how long
you can afford it before you start, because dual-run periods extend by default
and nobody ever volunteers to end one.

## Moving the data

Bandwidth arithmetic decides more than product preference. Take the volume, take
the usable bandwidth after production traffic, and divide. Then check the answer
against the business deadline and against how fast the data changes — a copy
that takes six weeks against a dataset with a daily delta needs a
synchronisation strategy, not just a transfer.

**Storage Transfer Service** moves data over the network from other clouds, from
on-premises filesystems, or between buckets, with scheduling and verification.
**Transfer Appliance** is physical shipment for volumes the wire cannot carry in
time. **Datastream** provides change data capture so a database can be
replicated continuously and cut over with minimal downtime, and it is also how
you keep an analytical copy current during a long transition.

## The programme, not the project

Exam guide section 4 covers analysing and optimising technical and business
processes, and Google's own path barely touches it. Expect questions where the
correct answer is organisational rather than technical, and expect the
technically impressive option to be wrong.

Know who signs off a cutover before the window opens, and know that the sign-off
is a named person with authority to accept the risk, not a committee. Agree
**rollback criteria** in advance: the specific measurable conditions under which
you revert, decided while everyone is calm rather than at 03:00 with the
business on a bridge call.

Change management is a deliverable. Users need to know what changes and when,
support teams need runbooks for the new stack before it carries traffic, and
training is a scheduled dependency with a duration, not an assumption. A plan
that moves the workload without moving the operational capability has moved the
outage rather than prevented it.

When a scenario describes a past incident and asks what to do next, the exam
usually wants root cause analysis and a process change, not an immediate
re-architecture. Rebuilding the system before understanding why it failed is
the answer that looks decisive and is wrong.

## Exam traps

A constraint that the existing stack must not change rules out refactoring
however elegant the target. Read for support matrices, vendor certification,
and "cannot be modified".

Datacentre exit dates make rehost correct even where replatform is better
engineering. Modernise after landing, from a position where the clock is yours.

Lift and shift of an over-provisioned estate carries the waste across.
Rightsizing during the move is the correct refinement, not a reason to reject
the strategy.

Wave plans built around team boundaries rather than dependency clusters
generate hybrid traffic and latency nobody budgeted for.

The right answer to a process question is often a conversation: a stakeholder
sign-off, a communication plan, a post-incident review. Technical answers to
organisational questions are distractors.

## Recap

- Selection follows time, change tolerance, and team capability far more often
  than technical merit
- Discover before you plan; size the target from actual utilisation, not from
  the current specification
- Wave by dependency cluster, easiest workload first, and know how long you can
  fund a dual run
- Continuous replication converts a migration outage into a cutover window;
  agree rollback criteria before opening it
- Section 4 questions want process answers. Root cause analysis beats immediate
  re-architecture

## Architecture drill

A university has 600 servers across two campus datacentres. One is closing in
sixteen months; the other stays. The estate includes a student records system
supported by a vendor on a fixed version, a research computing cluster used by
forty academics who each believe their workflow is unique, a library catalogue
last updated in 2011 whose supplier no longer exists, and around 200 servers
that nobody has been able to attribute to any department. Term time forbids
changes between September and December.

Decide before reading on:

1. What happens to the 200 unattributed servers?
2. How do the change-freeze months affect a sixteen-month plan?
3. Which workloads move, and with which strategy?
4. What goes in wave one?

**Discussion.** The unattributed servers are the most valuable finding in the
inventory. Some fraction of them are genuinely dead and retiring them is the
cheapest win available — no migration, no target, no licence. But you cannot
switch off what you cannot attribute, so the answer is evidence: network flow
data over a full cycle, including the March processes nobody mentions. Anything
with no inbound traffic across a term is a retirement candidate; anything with
traffic has just found its owner.

The change freeze removes four of sixteen months, so the plan has twelve working
months, not sixteen. Say that out loud early, because plans that quietly assume
the freeze is negotiable fail in October when it turns out it is not.

The records system rehosts — vendor version support removes every other option.
The library catalogue with no supplier is the interesting case: no vendor means
no support matrix to violate, but also nobody to fix what breaks, which argues
for the lowest-change option available. Rehost it and treat any modernisation as
a separate decision. Research computing is a replatform conversation, and forty
academics with unique workflows is a stakeholder problem before it is a
technical one.

Wave one is neither. It is the landing zone, then something dull with a
forgiving audience — the internal room-booking tool, not the records system in
enrolment week.
