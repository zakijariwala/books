# Chapter 10: Security, The Perimeter is Dead

A customer forwarded a link to a colleague, the colleague opened it, and the document appeared. This was the intended behaviour, and it was also the incident.

The colleague did not work at the customer's company. He had left eight months earlier and was now at a competitor, and the customer had forwarded the link to his personal address by accident, having typed three letters and accepted what the mail client offered. The link worked because the link was the permission. Anyone holding it could open the document, which was a deliberate design decision taken years earlier by people who wanted sharing to be easy, and which nobody had revisited because it had never caused a problem before.

Nothing was broken into. No password was guessed. No system failed, no alarm fired, and no dashboard turned red. A person who should not have seen a document saw it, using the front door, exactly as designed.

The question the company's leadership asked afterwards was the natural one and the wrong one. Is our system secure? There is no answer to that question. It has the grammar of a question about a state and it is asked about a thing that has no such state, and every honest person who has ever been asked it has felt the sentence go soft in their mouth.

This chapter is about the question that replaces it.

## The business problem

Security arrives at a business reader's desk in three forms, and all three are made harder by asking the unanswerable question.

It arrives as **the questionnaire**, three hundred rows of it, sent by a customer's procurement team, and somebody has to answer questions that assume a shape of company you may not be. Answering them well is a sales skill.

It arrives as **the incident**, which is rarer and is where the unanswerable question does its damage. In the hours after something has happened, the useful questions are all specific: what was reachable, by whom, for how long, and what did they take. A company that has only ever thought in terms of being secure or not secure has no vocabulary ready for any of that.

And it arrives as **the decision**, constantly, in the form of small trade-offs between convenience and control. Should links work for anyone holding them. Should support staff be able to see customer data. Should the analytics team have a copy of the production records. Each of those is a business judgement wearing technical clothing, each gets decided by whoever is in the room, and their accumulation is the real security posture of the company, whatever the questionnaire says.

The reason this chapter belongs in a book for business readers, rather than being left to specialists, is that the third form outweighs the other two. The questionnaire describes the company on paper. The incident tests it once. The decisions happen every week, in rooms full of people weighing customer convenience against a risk none of them has been given a way to describe, and they compound in the same direction as everything else in this book: toward whatever is easiest at the time.

## The wall is gone

The old picture was a building with a wall around it.

Inside the wall was the company: its offices, its staff, its machines, its data. Outside was everything else. The security job was to control the boundary, to make the wall high and the gate well guarded, and the working assumption was that anything already inside was legitimate. Get through the gate and you were among friends.

That model matched the room from chapter one. There was a door that took two cards. The machines were behind it. The staff were in the building. The wall was a real wall, and it corresponded to something.

Four things dismantled it, and none of them were security decisions.

**The machines moved into somebody else's building.** Chapter three's landlord model has your data in a warehouse you cannot walk into, reachable over public roads, and no wall you build can enclose it.

**The staff left the office.** People work from homes, trains, and other countries, on their own connections and sometimes their own devices. A boundary that treats being in the building as evidence of legitimacy stops meaning anything when the building is empty.

**The product became a collection of parts talking to each other.** Chapter two's six stops are separate services, and they communicate across the same kinds of roads that everyone else uses. Traffic that would once have been internal, and therefore trusted, is now traffic.

**And the company took on suppliers.** Every convenience adopted in chapter nine is another party with a connection into something of yours.

What is left is a company whose people, machines, data, and suppliers are distributed across the world, connected by public infrastructure. There is no inside. There is a set of things that need to reach each other, and the only useful question is which ones, under what conditions, and how far each can go once it is there.

That last clause is the chapter.

The replacement idea has a name in the industry and the name is not worth learning, because the substance is one sentence: **stop treating location as evidence.** Being on the office network, or inside the company's own systems, or already past some gate, tells you nothing about whether a request is legitimate. So every request proves who it is, every time, wherever it comes from, and the proof is checked against what that identity is allowed to do.

Stated that way it sounds like an inconvenience, and for a while it was. What makes it tolerable is that the proving is done by machines rather than people, which is why most of it is invisible to staff beyond the occasional prompt on a phone. The business consequence is that a company gets to stop pretending its boundary means something, which is worth more than the boundary was.

It also disposes of a category of reasoning that used to be common and is now dangerous. Anything that begins "that component is only reachable from inside our network, so it does not need to be careful" is an argument from the old picture. The office is empty, the network reaches homes and suppliers, and inside is a description of a diagram rather than of the world.

## Blast radius

Ships have watertight compartments. It is not because the hull is expected to hold. It is because the hull is expected, eventually, not to hold, and the design question is what happens next.

The naval architect's question is not whether the ship can be holed. It is: **when a compartment floods, how far does the water get?** That question has an answer, the answer can be improved with design, and improving it is a different discipline from making the hull thicker.

**Blast radius** is the same question asked about a company's systems. When one thing is taken, what else does that thing open?

The reason this question is better than the unanswerable one is that it can be answered, specifically, box by box, in an afternoon, by people who already know. For each part of the system: if somebody controlled this, what could they reach from here? Not what should they reach. What could they.

The answers tend to be worse than everyone expects, and the reason is understandable rather than negligent. Permissions are granted when something needs to be done, under time pressure, by somebody who wants to stop being blocked. Granting broad access solves the immediate problem and every narrower alternative takes longer to work out. Nobody comes back later to tighten it, because nothing is broken and tightening carries a risk of breaking something. This is the same accumulating ratchet as chapter five's undeleted data and chapter six's uncounted round trips, and it produces the same result: a state nobody chose, arrived at by a series of individually sensible steps.

The principle that counters it has an unhelpful technical name and a plain business meaning: **each part of the system should be able to reach only what its job requires.** The service that resizes photographs needs to read one file and write another. It does not need to read customer records, and if it can, then a flaw in image resizing has become a route to the customer database. That is a compartment with a hole in the bulkhead, and it is how a small compromise becomes an announcement.

The same idea applies to people. Support staff need to see enough to help a customer. Whether that includes everything about every customer is a decision somebody should make on purpose, because the alternative is that the answer is yes by default and stays that way until an incident makes it interesting.

There is a second half to containment that gets less attention and matters as much: **how long before anyone notices.** A compartment that floods and is spotted in minutes is an incident. The same compartment flooding unnoticed for four months is a different event entirely, with different obligations to customers and regulators, and the difference is not in the defences but in whether anybody was watching.

This is where the honest answer for most companies is uncomfortable. Attacks are rarely dramatic and rarely fast. Somebody with access to something small looks around, and looking around resembles ordinary use, because it is ordinary use performed by the wrong person. The signal that distinguishes them is that a component started reaching for things it has no business reaching for, which a company can only notice if somebody has written down what its business is.

Which returns the argument to the same place. Deciding what each part should reach is not only how you limit the spread. It is the only way to recognise the spread while it is happening, because without that decision there is no such thing as unusual.

A third element completes it, and it is the one a business reader can push hardest on: **how quickly can we shut a door?** When something is known to be compromised, the useful measure is how long it takes to revoke a credential, cut off a supplier's access, or invalidate every link issued before a certain moment. Companies that have never tried this discover during an incident that a permission granted in an afternoon takes a week to withdraw, because it was copied into places nobody kept track of.

## The photograph has a URL

Go back to the wedding photograph, because it makes the whole chapter concrete.

The photo sits on a shelf in a warehouse, and it has an address. Something has to decide who is allowed to fetch it, and there are two broad arrangements.

The first is that the address itself is the permission. Anyone who has the link can see the photograph. This is simple, it is fast, it costs nothing, and it makes sharing effortless. It also means the permission travels with the link, forever, to anybody it is ever forwarded to, screenshotted into, or pasted anywhere. There is no way to know who holds it.

The second is that every request must prove who it is, and the system checks whether that person is allowed. This costs a little work on every single view, which sounds trivial and is not, since the preview from chapter two is fetched every time anyone scrolls past. And it gives you something the first arrangement cannot: the ability to know who saw what, and to stop them later.

Neither is correct in general. A public marketing image should use the first. A medical record should use the second. Most companies apply one of them to everything, because it was decided once when the product was small, and the decision was made on grounds of convenience by people who were not thinking about the case where a customer forwards a link to a former colleague at a competitor.

Which is the incident from the opening, explained. Not a breach. A design decision, taken years earlier for good reasons, meeting a situation nobody had imagined. Most security incidents at ordinary companies look far more like this than like anything in a film.

There is a third arrangement worth knowing about, because it is what a thoughtful product offers and what a business reader can ask for. The link works for anyone holding it, and it stops working after a period, or after a number of views, or when somebody revokes it. This keeps most of the convenience and removes the permanence, which is the part that caused the trouble. A link that would have expired in a fortnight would have made the opening incident a non-event, and the cost of that feature is small and entirely predictable.

The reason it often does not exist is that expiry is a product decision nobody asked for. Customers do not request it, because customers do not think in these terms until something goes wrong. It has to be proposed by somebody inside the company who has thought about who holds the link, which is not a technical insight and does not come from an engineer's backlog.

The same question extends past documents to everything the product hands out. Invitations to join a workspace, password reset messages, links in emails, addresses that expose a report: each is a thing that grants access to whoever holds it, each was designed by somebody solving a convenience problem, and each has a lifetime that was either chosen or defaulted. Asking which is a good use of an afternoon.

## What Meera does with this

Meera stops asking whether the system is secure, and asks three questions that have answers.

The first, at design time: **"If this component were taken over, what else could it reach?"** She should ask it about the component being discussed, not about the company. The answer is a list, the list is usually longer than intended, and shortening it is ordinary work that somebody can schedule.

The second, about the thing she owns: **"Who can see this data, and is that on purpose?"** Not who should. Who can. Support, analytics, contractors, the vendor whose tool holds a copy, and anyone holding a link. Every one of those is a business decision that was probably made by default.

The third, for incidents: **"What was reachable, by whom, for how long?"** Those are the questions that produce a customer notification anyone can trust, and they are the same three questions a regulator will ask.

There is a fourth that belongs to her particularly, because it is a product decision that gets left to engineers. **"Should this be shareable by link, and for how long?"** Convenience and control trade against each other in a way only somebody who understands the customer can weigh. Links that expire, links that require a login, links that work for anyone: these are product features with security consequences, and treating them as an engineering detail is how the opening incident happens.

And there is a defensive use for the questionnaire. When a customer's procurement team sends three hundred rows, the company that can describe its containment, what each part can reach and what stops a compromise spreading, is answering a better question than the one asked. That answer travels well in security reviews, because the people reading them have seen a great many completed questionnaires and almost no clear explanations.

## Inside a real organization

The pattern is that the compromise is rarely the interesting part. The spread is.

Something small gives way: a credential in the wrong place, a supplier's tool, an account belonging to somebody who left, a permission granted for an afternoon three years ago. That first step is almost never sophisticated. What determines whether it becomes an incident or an announcement is entirely what that first foothold could reach, which was decided long before by people doing something else.

The second pattern is that nobody owns the accumulation. Access is granted by many people over many years, each for a good reason, and no single person has a picture of the total. Companies discover this when somebody finally builds the map, and the reaction is consistent: nobody is surprised by any individual grant and everybody is surprised by the sum.

The third connects back to chapter three, and it is where business readers can do genuine damage or genuine good. The landlord secures the building. What you put in your unit, and who you give keys to, is yours. Companies that believe their landlord's certifications cover them are misreading the contract, and the most common serious incident in this field remains a tenant leaving something open and assuming the building's guard covered it. The certifications are real and they describe the building.

The fourth is that security work is judged like reliability work in chapter nine: invisible when it succeeds, urgent for a quarter after a failure, and aimed at whatever went wrong last. Containment is the exception worth arguing for, because unlike most defensive work it pays out against attacks nobody predicted. You do not have to guess how they get in to have decided how far they get.

The fifth is about people leaving, and it is the most reliably neglected thing in this chapter. Every company has a process for granting access on the first day and something vaguer for the last one. Accounts persist, links keep working, a personal device still holds a copy, and a shared credential that three people knew is now known by two employees and one former employee. None of this requires anyone to behave badly. The opening incident of this chapter involved a man who had done nothing at all except still exist in a contacts list.

The sixth connects to chapter five, and it is the argument that most often lands with a finance-minded audience. Data you no longer hold cannot be exposed. Every record kept beyond its usefulness is both a line on the storage bill and a thing that can appear in a disclosure notice. The deletion policy that chapter five recommended for cost reasons is the same policy that limits the size of the worst day, which means it has two owners who have never discussed it and one obvious champion in whoever reads both chapters.

## The misconception

The belief is that security is a wall, and that a company is either behind one or not.

This is the picture that produces the unanswerable question, and it fails for a reason that has nothing to do with how good anyone's walls are. **There is no inside any more.** The staff are at home, the machines are in somebody else's building, the parts of the product talk to each other across public roads, and a dozen suppliers hold pieces of your data. A boundary can still be drawn, and boundaries remain useful, but a boundary is no longer the thing that determines what happens to you.

What determines what happens to you is containment. **Security is not a wall around the company. It is a set of compartments inside it.** The measure of a company's position is not whether anyone can get in, because given enough time and enough suppliers somebody eventually will, in some small way, through something nobody was watching. The measure is how far they get, how fast anyone notices, and how much is reachable from wherever they land.

That reframing changes what a business reader should ask for and what she should be sceptical of. A plan that consists of making the perimeter stronger is a plan built on the old picture. A plan that includes what each part can reach, what happens when one part is compromised, and how anyone would find out, is built on the current one.

And it changes what "we take security seriously" has to mean. Said with the wall picture, it means we spend money on defence. Said with the compartment picture, it means we have decided in advance how bad our worst day can be. Only the second is a claim with anything behind it.

There is a related belief worth dismantling while we are here, because it does as much damage as the wall. It is that security is a department, and that having one means the question is handled. A department can write the rules, run the reviews, and hold the map. It cannot make the hundred small trade-offs that happen in product meetings it is not invited to, and those trade-offs are where a company's real position is set. Whether links expire, whether support sees everything, whether the analytics team gets a copy of production data: none of those will be decided by a security team, and all of them determine what the worst day looks like.

Which is the argument for a business reader learning any of this at all. The technical work belongs to specialists and always will. The decisions do not, and the decisions are made in rooms where the specialists are absent, by people who have been taught to think of security as a wall somebody else is building.

For a reader with no company to defend, the same shift is worth applying at home. The useful question is not whether your accounts can be broken into, which is not in your control. It is what somebody reaches if one of them is: whether the same password opens the others, whether your email can reset everything else, and how much sits behind whichever account you protect least. That is a blast radius question, it takes an evening to answer, and it is the only version of this chapter's advice that anyone can act on alone.

## Summary

Three things to keep.

The wall is gone, and it did not fall to an attack. It was dismantled by the machines moving into rented buildings, the staff leaving the office, the product becoming parts that talk over public roads, and the suppliers accumulating. There is no inside to defend.

Blast radius replaces it, and it is the question that has an answer: when one thing is taken, what else does it open? Each part should reach only what its job requires, and the reason it usually reaches further is a series of reasonable grants nobody came back to tighten.

And the question to stop asking is whether the system is secure. The questions to ask instead are what this component could reach if taken, who can see this data and whether that is deliberate, and, on the bad day, what was reachable, by whom, for how long.

## The Monday question

Pick one component of your product and ask what it could reach if somebody controlled it. Then ask who granted that reach and when. The answer to the second question is almost always a person solving a different problem in a hurry, some years ago.
