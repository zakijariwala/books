# Chapter 3: Meet the Landlords

The call was going well until the client's head of IT leaned toward the speakerphone and asked a plain question. "Where does our data physically sit? Does it ever leave the country?"

There was a pause. Not a long one. Half a second. The account lead, who had been fluent for forty minutes about uptime and roadmaps, looked across the table at the solutions engineer. It was a small movement, the kind you make without deciding to.

The client saw it.

That look cost more than the pause did. The head of IT did not need the answer in that moment; she was going to get it in writing either way. What she needed to know was whether the company selling to her understood its own product, and the glance told her it did not. The rest of the call stayed warm. The deal slowed by a quarter anyway, and nobody could point to the sentence where it went wrong, because it was not a sentence. It was a look.

That half-second is the most expensive moment in enterprise software, and it comes from a belief almost everyone holds and almost nobody examines: that data in the cloud is nowhere in particular.

It is somewhere in particular. It is always somewhere in particular. Follow the photo from the last chapter and you can point at the somewhere on a map. The heavy file went onto a shelf in a warehouse. That warehouse stands in a specific business park, in a specific city, in a specific country, and somebody signed a contract that put it there. Nothing about the upload was abstract. The only abstract thing was the diagram.

## The business problem

The residency question arrives in three places, and all three cost money.

It arrives on sales calls, where it is a trust test rather than a technical query. The client's head of IT is establishing whether the person opposite understands what they are selling. Answer it plainly and the conversation moves on. Glance at the engineer and the room quietly downgrades everyone on your side of the table.

It arrives in procurement and audit, where it is a document rather than a conversation. Somebody has to state, in writing, which country holds the customer's records and under whose jurisdiction they sit. If nobody in the company knows, that answer gets guessed by whoever is closest to the form, and a guess written into a compliance document is a liability with a signature on it.

And it arrives after something has gone wrong, which is the expensive version. A regulator asks, a customer's lawyer asks, or a journalist asks. By then the answer is whatever it has been for the last four years, and nobody gets to choose it retroactively.

The pattern across all three is the same. The residency question is never hard to answer once somebody knows. It is hard to answer in the moment, in front of people, by someone who has never been told. That distinction is worth holding onto, because it means the fix is not technical training. It is a single afternoon of asking, written down, kept current.

None of these require you to be technical. All of them require you to know what a region is, who your landlord is, and what you signed.

## Three questions a tenant asks

Your company did not buy a building. It rented from a landlord.

That was the trade in chapter one: give up the room you could touch, get the freedom to grow on a Tuesday and shrink on a Wednesday. What that chapter did not cover is what kind of relationship you entered, and a tenancy is a relationship with terms.

A real tenant, a person renting a flat, asks three things before signing. Which city is it in. Who fixes what when something breaks. And how hard will it be to move out. Companies sign cloud leases every week without asking any of the three, and the three map exactly onto the questions that show up later in audits, outages, and board meetings.

### One: which country is the building in?

The landlords own warehouses in clusters, and the industry calls each cluster a **region**. A region is a city, in the sense that matters: a place on a map, with a climate, a power grid, a legal system, and a government. When your team set the company up, somebody chose one from a list.

Inside a region, the landlord runs several separate buildings, far enough apart that a flood or a power failure in one does not take the others down, close enough that they can pass data between themselves at speed. The industry calls these **availability zones**. For your purposes they are separate loading docks at the same port. They matter for reliability, which is a later chapter. They do not change the answer to the residency question, because they all sit in the same country.

This nesting is the whole picture, and it is worth being able to draw on a napkin. A country contains regions. A region contains availability zones. An availability zone contains the building your file is in. When somebody says the system runs in three zones, they have told you about resilience and nothing at all about jurisdiction. When somebody names the region, they have told you the country, and the country is what the lawyer wants.

Go back to the photograph from the last chapter. The guest tapped share at a wedding, and the heavy file landed on a shelf. That shelf sits in a building, in a zone, in a region, in a country, and the reason it landed in that country rather than another is that somebody at the company running the app picked an entry from a list, probably in a hurry, possibly years before the guest ever installed the thing. Her photograph is now subject to the laws of a nation she did not choose and could not name. That is not a scandal either. It is a fact about the world, and she has no way of learning it.

The country is the part that matters, and here is why it is not a technicality. **The building obeys the law of the land it stands on, not the law of the land you sit in.** If your customers' records live in a warehouse in a particular nation, then that nation's courts, its police, and its rules about what a company can be compelled to hand over all reach into the space you are renting. Your landlord follows local law, because the alternative is that the landlord's staff go to prison.

This is not a scandal, and it is not a flaw in the cloud. It is how buildings work. A storage unit in one city does not get to follow the rules of a city three borders away because the renter would prefer it.

Two consequences follow, and they pull in opposite directions. The first is that some countries require certain categories of information, health records and government data being the usual examples, to stay inside their borders. If you sell into those markets, the region choice stops being an engineering decision and becomes a condition of the sale. The second is that a company headquartered in one country may be subject to that country's demands for data it holds elsewhere. Which means the flag over the warehouse and the flag over the landlord's head office are two separate questions, and a thorough client will ask both.

So when the head of IT asks whether the data leaves the country, she is not asking a technical question wearing technical clothes. She is asking whose government can reach her customers' information and whether her own regulator will accept the arrangement. That is a business question, and the person who can answer it plainly is the person the room begins to trust.

### Two: who fixes what when it breaks?

The second tenant question has a name in this industry, **shared responsibility**, and it is the source of more unpleasant surprises than any other idea in this book.

The split is roughly this. The landlord is responsible for the building: the walls, the power, the cooling, the physical security of the site, and the machines themselves. You are responsible for what you put inside your unit and who you give keys to. The landlord will keep the roof on. The landlord will not notice that you left your unit unlocked, and if you do, the contract says that part was yours.

Most companies understand this in principle and misjudge where the line falls in practice. They assume that because the landlord is enormous and competent and certified, security is handled. What is handled is the building. The contents are still yours, and the most common serious incident in this field is not a landlord failing. It is a tenant leaving a door open and assuming the building's guard covered it.

The reason the line is easy to misjudge is that it moves depending on what you rent. Rent a bare machine and almost everything above the metal is yours: the operating system, the patching, the configuration, who can log in. Rent a managed database instead and the landlord takes over the patching and the backups, and your share shrinks to the data and the permissions. Rent a finished service and your share shrinks again. Each step up hands more responsibility to the landlord and costs more per unit of work, and this is a real business trade rather than a technical preference. You are buying back staff time, and paying for it on the invoice instead of on the payroll.

What nobody tells you is that the line moves without announcement as your teams adopt different products. A company can be a careful tenant in one part of the building and a careless one in another, and the contract is the same document throughout. When somebody asks whether your infrastructure is secure, the honest first answer is a question: which part, and who is holding that part.

There is a second edge to this that shows up during outages. When the power fails in a rented warehouse, the phone that rings belongs to somebody you will never meet, employed by the landlord, whose entire job is that the room stays cold. That is the good part of the deal, and chapter one argued it is the best part. The cost is that during those hours you have no lever. You cannot go to the room, you cannot prioritise your own recovery, and you will find out what happened when the landlord publishes an explanation. You traded the pager for a seat in the waiting room.

### Three: how hard is it to move out?

The third question is the one that gets asked last and should be asked first, and it is where the phrase **lock-in** belongs.

Moving out of a flat is tedious and finite. You hire a van. Moving a company off a landlord is a different shape of problem, because you did not just put boxes in the unit. You built around the shelving.

The landlords all rent the same basic goods: machines, storage, network. If that were all you used, leaving would be an inconvenience. But every landlord also sells conveniences on top, the things that make a team faster, and each convenience is shaped a little differently from the equivalent next door. The queue works this way here and that way there. The permissions model is not the same. The managed database has its own habits. Every time a team takes one of those, they save a month now and add a week to the eventual move.

None of that is a trick. The conveniences are genuine, the time saved is real, and a company that refuses all of them to stay portable pays for that purity every single day in exchange for an exit it may never take.

It helps to know which parts move and which do not, because the fear is usually aimed at the wrong thing. The data moves. It is tedious and it costs money to shift in bulk, for reasons chapter five explains, but data is portable and every landlord will help you bring yours in. The plain machines move; a program that runs on a rented computer here runs on a rented computer there. What does not move is everything written against a particular landlord's conveniences, plus the harder thing nobody counts: your people's knowledge. A team that has spent four years learning one landlord's quirks is a team that will be slower for a year in a new building, and that year does not appear in anyone's migration estimate.

What makes lock-in worth naming is that it accumulates without anyone deciding. No meeting is ever held about it. Each individual choice is correct, and after four years the cost of leaving has quietly become a number nobody has calculated and everybody assumes is somebody else's problem. The exit cost is real whether or not it is written down, and chapter eleven puts it on the bill where it belongs.

There is a version of this conversation that goes badly, and it is worth avoiding. Somebody senior reads an article, arrives at a meeting, and asks whether the company is locked in. The room hears an accusation, gets defensive, and the discussion becomes about whether past decisions were wise. The useful version asks a different question: if we had to leave within a year, what would break, what would we rewrite, and who would we have to hire? That question can be answered in an afternoon by people who are not on trial, and the answer is a number the board can hold.

## What Meera does with this

Meera does not need to become an engineer to answer the residency question. She needs three sentences, and she can get them from her own team this week.

The first: **which country does our customer data physically live in right now?** Not which vendor. The country. If the answer takes more than a day to obtain, that is itself a finding worth reporting.

The second: **whose laws and whose government can therefore reach it?** This follows from the first and is the part the client's head of IT was reaching for.

The third: **if a customer requires their data to stay inside their own borders, can we put it there, and what does that cost us?** This one is a product question dressed as an infrastructure question, and it belongs to her more than to anyone else in the building. The answer is usually yes, at the price of running the product in more than one place, which costs more and complicates everything downstream. That trade is hers to weigh.

With those three answers written down, the next call goes differently. The head of IT asks where the data sits. Meera answers, names the country, names what that means for jurisdiction, and says what the options are if the client needs something else. She does not look at the solutions engineer. The glance is the tell; the plain answer is the trust.

The part worth rehearsing is what to do when the honest answer is unwelcome. Sometimes the data sits in a country the client would rather it did not. The instinct is to soften it, to say the architecture is global, to reach for the mist. That is the answer that loses the deal slowly, because the client's head of IT has asked this question of eleven vendors and can hear the difference between a fact and a fog.

The better move is to say the country, say why it was chosen or admit it was inherited, and go straight to the options and what they cost. "Today it is in this country. That was set before either of us was involved. If your policy requires it to stay inside your borders, we can run you in a region that does, it takes this long, and here is what changes for you." Nobody has ever lost a deal by knowing where their own product runs. Deals are lost by the pause.

There is also a defensive use, and Meera will need it sooner than she expects. When a client asks for residency in a country the company does not serve, somebody in sales will want to say yes. The three answers give her the standing to say what yes would involve: a second place to run everything, a second set of things that can break, a second bill, and a release process that has to keep two places in step. That is not a refusal. It is the price, offered early enough to be a decision rather than a discovery.

There is a fourth question she can now ask internally, and it is the one that changes decisions rather than conversations. When somebody proposes serving a new market, she can ask which region that market's data would live in, whether that is a new region for the company, and what running in two places does to the cost and to the release process. That question arrives at the beginning of a plan rather than in the middle of an audit, which is worth a great deal and costs nothing.

## Inside a real organization

The uncomfortable truth about the residency answer is that in most companies it was never decided. It was defaulted.

Somebody, years ago, set up an account to test something. The console offered a region already selected, because a list has to have something at the top. That person took the default, meaning to move it later, and later did not arrive. The test became a staging environment, the staging environment became production, and the company's customers have been living in a country nobody chose ever since. When the residency question finally arrives, the honest answer to "why does our data live there" is not a strategy. It is that the option was already highlighted, and it stuck.

The second thing worth knowing about real organizations is that the residency answer is rarely one answer. The customer's records may sit in one country while the backups sit in another, the logs go somewhere a monitoring vendor chose, and a support tool holds copies of whatever a customer pasted into a ticket. Each of those was a reasonable decision made by a different team on a different Tuesday. Together they mean the honest answer to "does our data ever leave the country" is often "most of it does not," which is not the answer anyone wants to give and is frequently the true one.

Finding out is not a technical exercise. It is a list of vendors and a set of questions, and it is the kind of work that gets done properly only when someone with authority asks for it before a client does.

The third thing is organizational, and it explains why the answer is so often missing. Nobody's job title contains this question. The infrastructure team knows which regions the company runs in. The legal team knows what the contracts promise. The sales team knows what has been said on calls. Support knows what its tooling retains. Each group holds a quarter of the answer and assumes one of the others holds the whole thing. The question only gets assembled when somebody outside all four asks it, which is why it usually arrives from a client.

That is the strongest argument for a product manager owning it. Not because she knows more than the infrastructure team, but because she is the only person who talks to all four groups in a normal week, and the answer is an assembly job rather than a research job.

## The misconception

The belief underneath all of this is that the cloud is everywhere, and that everywhere means nowhere. People picture their data floating in a jurisdiction-free mist, subject to no country in particular.

The brochures encourage it. They say **global** in a tone suggesting your information has transcended geography, joined a kind of international waters where no flag flies. It has not. Global means the landlord has warehouses in many countries and yours is sitting in one specific building in one specific one of them, under one specific flag, within reach of one specific courthouse.

Global is not the absence of a place. It is a place you were never told to look at.

The correction matters because the mist version produces bad decisions that feel like no decision at all. If data is nowhere, the region dropdown is a formality and the default is fine. If data is somewhere, that dropdown is a jurisdictional choice with legal consequences, made once, hard to reverse, and worth five minutes of a serious person's attention. Same click. Two entirely different acts.

For a reader with no stake in any of this, the correction is worth more than it looks. Your own photographs, messages, medical records, and bank statements are sitting in buildings, in countries, under governments, and the terms of that arrangement were set by companies choosing from lists. You are a tenant of a tenant, and the mist is the reason it never occurred to you to ask. When a news story breaks about one government demanding access to data held by a company headquartered in another, this is the machinery underneath it: not an abstraction about the internet, but a warehouse standing on somebody's soil, staffed by people who live under somebody's laws.

## Summary

Three things to keep.

Your company is a tenant, and a tenancy has terms. The three questions a tenant asks are which country the building is in, who fixes what when it breaks, and what it costs to move out. Those three cover most of what will ever be asked of you about your infrastructure.

The building follows the law of the land it stands on. A region is a place with a government, an availability zone is a separate building in that same place, and the country on the map is the answer to the residency question. If nobody at your company can name that country, that is the finding.

And lock-in is not a trap anyone set. It accumulates one sensible convenience at a time, without a meeting, until leaving has a price nobody has calculated. It is worth calculating before somebody asks, and the calculation is cheap compared to the alternative, which is discovering the number during a negotiation with the landlord who benefits from it.

Next comes the inside of the building: what the machines are, what it means to rent one by the hour, and why the answer to "we need bigger servers" is sometimes an afternoon and sometimes a quarter.

## The Monday question

Ask someone on your team which country your customers' data physically sits in today, and whether that was chosen or inherited. Then ask the same question about your backups, your logs, and your support tooling, and see whether the four answers match.
