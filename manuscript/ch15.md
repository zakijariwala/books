# Case study: hybrid retailer with a generative catalogue

## The scenario

**Halverston & Co** sells homeware and garden equipment through a website, a
mobile application, and sixty-one physical stores. Roughly a third of revenue
arrives in the six weeks before Christmas and a second, smaller peak lands in
early spring.

The estate grew by acquisition and it shows. The web storefront runs on a
Kubernetes cluster in Google Cloud. Order management runs on SQL Server in the
company's own datacentre, as does the warehouse system, which exchanges files
with suppliers over SFTP on a nightly schedule. Product catalogue data sits in
MySQL. A document store holds product content — descriptions, specifications,
care instructions — and was inherited from an acquired brand. Redis fronts the
storefront for session and price lookups. A separate MySQL instance supports the
loyalty scheme, and nobody is sure why it is separate. An IVR system routes
calls to a contact centre of ninety agents, and it is on-premises hardware
approaching end of support. Monitoring is a third-party product the
infrastructure team has used for a decade.

The catalogue has 340,000 items. Around 90,000 have a description consisting of
the supplier's part name. Merchandisers write better copy by hand, at a rate
that clears about four hundred items a week, and the backlog grows faster than
that.

Stated requirements:

- Generate product descriptions and attribute tags automatically from supplier
  data and product images.
- **No catalogue content reaches the public site without a named merchandiser
  approving it.** Trading standards obligations apply to product claims, and the
  legal team owns this rule.
- Reduce total infrastructure cost. The board approved the AI programme on the
  condition that it does not increase the run rate.
- Payment card data is in scope for compliance audit, and the auditors want
  evidence of segregation.
- The storefront must absorb the December peak without the manual capacity
  planning currently done each November.
- The nightly supplier file exchange delays stock accuracy by up to
  twenty-four hours, and stores are selling items that are not there.

## Working the problem

**The approval requirement is the constraint sentence.** Any architecture that
publishes generated content automatically fails, regardless of how good the
generation is. Generated descriptions land in a review queue with the source
data and the image beside them; a merchandiser approves, edits, or rejects; only
approved content publishes. Record who approved what and when, because "a named
merchandiser" plus an audit obligation means the record is part of the
deliverable. Note also that this design turns merchandisers from authors into
reviewers, which is where the throughput gain actually comes from — four hundred
items a week written becomes several thousand reviewed.

**Database sprawl, consolidated with judgment.** The catalogue MySQL and the
loyalty MySQL have no reason to be separate; consolidating them onto Cloud SQL
for MySQL removes an instance and a backup regime. The document store holding
product content is a genuine document workload and Firestore fits it. Redis goes
to Memorystore. SQL Server for order management is the one to leave alone
initially — it is in the compliance scope, it is the system of record for money,
and moving it during the same programme as the AI work is how you get two risky
projects instead of one. Consolidation is a service-by-service argument, not a
policy, and the exam rewards the answer that leaves something where it is for a
stated reason.

**Replace the nightly file exchange.** Twenty-four-hour stock latency causing
oversells is a business problem with a direct architecture. Suppliers publish to
Pub/Sub, or a transfer lands files that trigger event-driven processing; either
way the batch window disappears. Design for at-least-once delivery, because
duplicate stock adjustments are worse than late ones. For the internal
databases, change data capture streams updates continuously rather than
extracting nightly.

**Generation and enrichment.** Attribute tagging from product images is a
prebuilt vision task — train nothing. Description writing is generative, and it
should be grounded in supplier specifications so claims trace to source data
rather than to the model's imagination, which matters when trading standards
apply. Run the 90,000-item backfill as batch. New products arriving is the
event-driven case.

**Compliance segregation.** Payment card scope is bounded by project structure
and network design: the cardholder environment in its own project, its own
subnets, its own service accounts, with a VPC Service Controls perimeter around
it and narrow, documented egress. Auditors want to see a boundary they can point
at, and hierarchy plus perimeter gives them one that IAM policy alone does not.

**The peak.** Manual November capacity planning is the finding. Regional GKE
with cluster autoscaling and horizontal pod autoscaling on a signal that
reflects load, plus a global external Application Load Balancer with Cloud CDN
carrying static product media. The load balancer's single anycast address also
removes the DNS-based regional stitching the team would otherwise build.

**Where cost reduction actually comes from.** Not from the AI. It comes from the
always-on footprint: committed use discounts against the baseline that runs
every day of the year, autoscaling so the December shape is rented rather than
owned, storage lifecycle rules on years of product imagery, and retiring the
duplicate database instances. The AI spend is controlled by batching the
backfill and choosing a model proportionate to the task, but it is a new cost
line, and the board's condition is met elsewhere.

## The tradeoff you defend

The efficient answer is to publish generated descriptions directly, with
sampling and quality monitoring afterwards. It clears 90,000 items in days
rather than months, and the quality would likely be acceptable.

It fails the requirement. Trading standards obligations attach to published
product claims, the legal team owns the rule, and "a named merchandiser
approves" is not a preference to be optimised away. Governance constraints are
constraints, and an architecture that meets every technical requirement while
breaking a stated approval obligation is the wrong architecture. Sampling after
publication catches errors after a customer has relied on one.

The second tradeoff is SQL Server. Leaving it on-premises through this
programme costs money and looks unfinished, and it is still correct: the
compliance boundary is drawn around it, it holds the money, and sequencing it
after the catalogue work is the difference between one project and two.

## What the exam would ask

Catalogue content must be reviewed before publication. Which answers does that
sentence eliminate, and what does the architecture need beyond the review queue
itself?

Stock accuracy lags twenty-four hours because of nightly file exchange. What
replaces it, and what property of the messaging layer must the design
accommodate?

The board requires that infrastructure cost not increase. Where does the saving
come from, given that the AI workload is new spend?

Auditors want evidence that payment card data is segregated. Which two
mechanisms provide the boundary, and why is IAM alone insufficient?
