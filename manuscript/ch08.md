# Chapter 8: Scaling, Surviving Black Friday

The campaign worked. That was the problem.

Marketing had spent four months on it, the budget was the largest the company had committed to a single push, and at eleven in the morning it went live across three channels at once. Traffic rose to four times normal in under two minutes. At two minutes and ten seconds the site stopped answering, and for the next nineteen minutes the most expensive advertisement in the company's history pointed several hundred thousand people at a page that would not load.

The post-mortem meeting was tense in the specific way those meetings are when nobody did anything wrong. Marketing had told engineering the campaign was coming. Engineering had confirmed the system scaled automatically. Both statements were true. What neither party had established was what "scales automatically" meant in minutes, and the answer turned out to be about six of them, which is four minutes and ten seconds too slow when the traffic arrives in two.

Somebody in that meeting asked the question this chapter is about. "What happens at ten times?" The honest answer was that nobody knew, because the company had never been at ten times and had no way to find out except by getting there.

## The business problem

A VP is going to ask you what the system does at ten times traffic, and there are three possible answers.

The first is "it holds," which is rarely true and should be interrogated whenever offered.

The second is "it falls over," which sounds like a failure and is a perfectly respectable answer if the company has decided that ten times is not worth building for. Most businesses will never see ten times. Paying to survive it every day of the year is the mistake chapter one spent its length describing.

The third answer is the one that separates companies that survive their big day from companies that hold a tense post-mortem. It goes: **"It sheds the following things in the following order, and here is what a customer sees at each stage."** That is not a claim about capacity. It is a plan, made in advance, about what to sacrifice.

The business stake is concentrated and asymmetric. The traffic that breaks a system is almost always the traffic you most wanted. The launch that worked. The mention that went further than expected. The sale. The day the campaign landed. Failing on an ordinary Tuesday costs you a Tuesday; failing at the peak costs you the event you spent four months and a budget creating, and it does so in front of the largest audience you will have that year.

The compounding version is worse. The audience at a peak is disproportionately made up of people meeting the product for the first time, because that is what a campaign or a mention delivers. Existing customers forgive an outage, having seen the product work. A first-time visitor who meets a page that will not load has met the product, and there is no second impression to correct it with. The cost of the nineteen minutes is therefore not nineteen minutes of lost revenue. It is a share of every future relationship that would have started that morning.

## What automatic scaling does, and how long it takes

The promise is simple and mostly true. When demand rises, the system adds capacity. When demand falls, it removes it. Chapter four established the requirement: this only works for things that can be copied, so the machines that hold nothing get to participate and the one desk that decides what is true does not.

The part that is left out of the promise is time, and time is the whole story.

Adding capacity is not a switch. It is a sequence, and each step takes a measurable while.

Something has to **notice** that demand has risen. Nothing watches continuously; systems sample, and a sample taken every minute cannot notice anything faster than a minute.

Then it has to **decide**, and deliberately not too fast, because a system that reacts to every twitch spends its life adding and removing capacity at some expense. So the rule usually says demand must stay high for a short period before anything happens.

Then a machine has to **start**, which takes as long as starting a computer takes, plus fetching the software, plus whatever the application does before it is ready to work. That last part is the one nobody accounts for, and it is often the largest. A program that has to read a lot into memory before it can answer anything is a program that is present but useless for a while.

Then the front door from chapter two has to **notice it is ready** and start sending it work.

Add those together and you get the reaction time, which for many systems is minutes rather than seconds. The number in the meeting above was six.

Here is the consequence in one sentence: **if traffic arrives faster than your reaction time, automatic scaling does not save you.** It saves you from the busy afternoon. It does not save you from the launch, the mention, or the ninety seconds after a match ends.

The shaded gap in that picture is the whole subject. It is not a gap in capacity, since capacity arrives; it is a gap in time, and the customers who arrive inside it get nothing. A company can have provisioned generously, spent well, and chosen good engineers, and still lose nineteen minutes because the arithmetic of noticing and starting does not care how much anyone spent.

Worse, the gap has a habit of widening itself under exactly the conditions that create it. The machines already running are struggling, which makes everything slower, which includes the work of starting new machines and telling the front door about them. Systems under sharp load get worse at recovering from load. This is why a site that is slightly overwhelmed often stays overwhelmed longer than the traffic that caused it, and why the recovery in that post-mortem took nineteen minutes rather than six.

## The ninety seconds after the match

Go back to the wedding photograph, and multiply it.

A match ends. In the ninety seconds that follow, four hundred thousand people do exactly what the wedding guest did: they take the thing they just captured and they push it up. Every one of those uploads takes the trip from chapter two. The road, the front door, the warehouse, the nine-second worker, the ledger.

Two things about that shape matter more than the volume.

The first is that it is not a rise. It is a step. Traffic does not climb toward the peak over an hour; the match ends and the peak exists. No reaction time survives that, which means anything that will be needed must already be running before the whistle.

The second is that the load is not evenly distributed across the six stops. The warehouse is unbothered; it was built for this and will accept four hundred thousand files without complaint. The nine-second workers multiply happily, because they hold nothing. The front door copes if it was sized for it. And the ledger, the one desk that must record that each photograph exists, receives four hundred thousand changes in ninety seconds and is the reason the whole thing either holds or does not.

Which produces the rule that governs every conversation about scale: **a system scales to its weakest link, and the weakest link is usually the part that cannot be copied.** Adding machines to a system whose constraint is the single write desk is a way of arriving at the bottleneck faster.

This is the most useful sentence to carry into a planning meeting, because it changes what a capacity conversation is about. The question stops being how much of everything to buy and becomes which single component is the constraint, which is a question with one answer that somebody can name. Every other component is either comfortable or irrelevant to the outcome, and money spent on those is money that will not change what happens on the day.

It also explains a pattern that otherwise looks like incompetence. A company invests in scaling, the investment is real, and the next event fails at the same point as the last one. The investment went to the parts that were easy to scale, because those were the parts where the work was tractable and the progress was visible. The constraint stayed where it was. Nobody was idle and nothing improved, which is the most demoralising kind of engineering quarter there is.

The reason peak events concentrate on the ledger rather than spreading out is worth one more sentence, because it is not obvious. Ordinary traffic is a mixture: people browsing, searching, reading, occasionally buying. Peak traffic is a monoculture. Everyone is doing the identical thing, which means the load lands on one path through the system rather than being spread across all of them, and the busiest stop on that one path is where the whole event is decided.

## Capacity that appears and disappears

There is an arrangement that sidesteps the reaction time for certain kinds of work, and the wedding photograph already showed it.

The nine-second worker that made the preview did not have to be started, warmed, or waited for. It came into existence because there was work, did the work, and stopped. Nobody had provisioned it in advance. Nobody scaled it. Four hundred thousand of them can exist in the same ninety seconds without anyone deciding anything, and then none of them exist and none of them cost anything.

The industry sells this as a category of product, and the name is not worth learning. What matters is the shape: **you are billed per piece of work rather than per hour of machine.** It suits work that arrives in bursts, finishes quickly, and holds nothing between one piece and the next. Resizing an image, sending a notification, processing an upload.

The trade is real and worth stating. Per unit of work it costs more than a machine you keep busy. If your work is steady, a running machine is the cheaper arrangement. If your work is spiky, paying per piece means the quiet hours cost nothing and the ninety seconds after the match cost what they cost, which is exactly the shape chapter one described wanting.

There is a second limitation, and it is the one that catches people. This arrangement scales the parts that hold nothing. It does not scale the ledger. Four hundred thousand instant workers all trying to write to the one desk is the same bottleneck arriving faster and in better order.

A third limitation is worth knowing before anyone proposes it as a general answer. A worker that has not run recently takes longer on its first piece of work, because it has to be brought into existence before it can begin. For work nobody is waiting on, this is irrelevant. For something a customer is watching, an occasional slow first response is a real cost, and it lands unpredictably on whichever unlucky person arrived after a quiet spell.

Which gives a clean division worth carrying. Work that happens behind the scenes, in bursts, holding nothing, suits paying per piece. Work a customer waits on, arriving steadily, suits a machine that stays warm. Most companies need both, and the mistake is choosing one arrangement for everything because it worked somewhere.

## What has to be decided in advance

This is the deliverable of the chapter: the things that cannot be worked out on the day, because on the day everyone is watching a graph and nobody is in a position to make a trade-off calmly.

**What gets sacrificed first.** When capacity is short, something has to give, and the choice should be made by people who understand the business. Recommendations, related items, activity feeds, live counts, personalisation: these are features that improve a good day and can be switched off during a bad ten minutes, and a page that loads without them is a page that loads. If nobody chose in advance, the system chooses for you by failing at whichever point it happens to break, and the customer loses the checkout rather than the recommendations.

**What may be made to wait.** Some work must happen now and some must merely happen. The order must be taken now. The confirmation email, the analytics, the search index update, the recommendation refresh: those can be queued and worked through over the following minutes, which is chapter seven's queue used deliberately. Deciding which is which is a business judgement.

**What is turned off entirely.** Reports, bulk exports, and internal tooling compete for the same ledger as the customers do. Many companies survive their peak by suspending things nobody outside the building would notice.

**Who is allowed to say stop.** During the nineteen minutes, somebody has to be able to decide to shed load without convening a meeting. If that authority is not assigned in advance it defaults to whoever is most senior and awake, which is a poor way to choose.

**What "already running" means.** For a step-shaped event, the reaction time is irrelevant and capacity must be in place beforehand. That is a decision with a cost attached, taken in advance, based on a forecast that may be wrong. Which is chapter one's problem in miniature: you are guessing at a peak and paying for the guess, except the guess covers hours rather than eighteen months.

The general name for this is graceful degradation, and the useful way to hold it is as a ranked list of what the business is willing to lose, in order. Any company can produce that list in an hour. Almost none have.

The reason almost none have is worth naming, because it is not laziness. Producing the list requires somebody to say out loud that a feature their colleague built is the first thing to be switched off when the company is under pressure. That is an uncomfortable sentence in a room where the person who built it is sitting, and it is far easier to leave the ranking unstated and let an outage do the ranking instead. An outage ranks by accident and blames nobody, which is organisationally convenient and commercially terrible.

The counter to that discomfort is to frame the list as what gets protected rather than what gets dropped. Every company can agree that customers must be able to complete a purchase, or send a message, or whatever the one irreplaceable act of the product is. Working outward from the thing that must survive is the same exercise with the politics removed, and it produces the same list.

There is one more decision that belongs on this list and is almost always missed. **What do we tell people?** During the nineteen minutes, several hundred thousand people met a page that would not load and formed a view. A holding page that says the company knows and is working on it converts an outage into an inconvenience, and it has to be written and deployable before the day, because nobody writes good copy while a graph is falling. The technical work of shedding load and the communication work of explaining it are the same project, and only one of them usually has an owner.

## What Meera does with this

Meera's question to engineering is not "will it scale." That question has no answer and invites reassurance.

Her question is: **"At ten times, what breaks first?"**

This one has an answer, and the answer is a specific box. If nobody can name it, that is the finding, and it means no one has thought past the reassurance. If somebody names it immediately, the conversation can proceed to what happens next.

Her second question is about time: **"How fast does our capacity react, and how fast does our traffic arrive?"** Two numbers, and only the relationship matters. Six-minute reaction against traffic that builds over an hour is fine. The same six minutes against a campaign or a match is an outage that has already been scheduled.

Her third is the one that produces the plan: **"What do we turn off first, and who decides?"** She should expect this to be unanswered the first time she asks. Asking it in September rather than in November is the difference between a decision and an incident.

There is also a way to make the whole exercise cheaper, and it belongs to her rather than to engineering. Traffic that arrives as a step can sometimes be persuaded to arrive as a slope. Releasing a sale to segments a few minutes apart, staggering notifications, opening a queue that admits people in order: each of these turns the hardest shape in this chapter into the easiest one, and each is a product decision costing nothing in infrastructure. A company that cannot afford to survive a step can often afford to avoid creating one.

And there is a question for her own side of the table, because engineering is not the only party who can prevent this. When marketing plans a campaign, somebody should tell engineering the shape as well as the date: how many people, arriving over how long, doing what. A campaign that drives readers to an article is a different load from one that drives them to sign up, which is a different load again from one that drives them to buy. The failure in the post-mortem above began as a communication gap and only became a technical event at two minutes and ten seconds.

## Inside a real organization

The pattern that catches companies is that they test the wrong thing.

Load testing, where it happens, tends to test the system as it is expected to be used: a realistic mixture of activity, ramped up smoothly, over a sensible period. Real traffic events are none of those. They are one activity, arriving as a step, all doing the identical thing at the identical moment, which is a different and much harsher shape. Four hundred thousand people all uploading is not four hundred thousand people using the product; it is the same request four hundred thousand times, concentrated on whichever stop handles that request.

The second pattern is that success is what breaks systems, and the organisation is therefore poorly arranged to prevent it. The people who create traffic events are rewarded for creating them. The people who absorb traffic events are rewarded for nothing visible when it goes well and blamed when it does not. Those two groups sit in different parts of the company and often meet for the first time during the post-mortem. A calendar shared between marketing and engineering, listing what is planned and how sharp each event will be, costs nothing and prevents more outages than most technical work.

The third pattern is that the fix after an incident is usually more capacity, which addresses the least likely cause. The company in this chapter did not lack machines. It lacked six minutes. Buying more machines that also take six minutes to arrive produces the same outcome at greater expense, and the meeting that approves it feels responsible.

The fourth pattern is what happens to the capacity afterwards. Following an incident, somebody raises the floor so that the system is permanently larger, on the reasonable grounds that this will not happen again. Nobody ever lowers it. The company then pays for the campaign's peak during every quiet Sunday for the following three years, and when somebody in finance asks about the increase two quarters later, the person who could explain it has moved teams. This is chapter four's four-times-bigger pattern with an incident attached, and the incident makes it harder to question, because unwinding it means being the person who reduced capacity before something went wrong.

The fifth is that the most reliable protection is the least technical. Companies that survive their peaks tend to be the ones where somebody sends a message saying the sale starts at eleven, expects this many people, and they will all do the same thing. That message is worth more than most of the engineering in this chapter, and it does not require anyone to understand any of it.

## The misconception

The belief is that the cloud scales automatically, and that this is what you are paying for.

It scales automatically in the sense that a thermostat heats a house automatically. It notices, it responds, and there is a delay between the two that is fine for weather and inadequate for a door being opened. Nobody would claim a thermostat can hold a room's temperature during a wall being removed. That is the promise being made about traffic.

The sharper correction is this. **Elasticity is a rate, not a capability.** The right question is never whether the system can reach the required size, because it usually can. It is whether it can get there before the customers give up, and that is a race between two speeds, one of which belongs to your infrastructure and one of which belongs to the internet.

And there is a second half people miss. Scaling down is also a rate, and it is the one that shows up on the bill. A system that expanded for the campaign and takes a day to relax is billing for the campaign long after the campaign. Companies notice the failure to scale up because customers complain. Nobody complains about the failure to scale down, so it persists for months and arrives as a mystery in chapter eleven.

For a reader with no system to defend, the same idea explains a familiar frustration. The ticket site that fails at the moment tickets are released is not a badly built site on an ordinary day. It is a site meeting a step-shaped event, where every person in the country arrives in the same ten seconds, and where the thing they all want is a single scarce record that one desk must decide about. That is the hardest shape in this book, and it is why those sites fail in the same way every time.

## Summary

Three things to keep.

Automatic scaling has a reaction time, measured in minutes, and if traffic arrives faster than that reaction time, it does not help. Elasticity is a rate rather than a capability, and the useful comparison is your reaction time against your traffic's arrival speed.

A system scales to its weakest link, and the weakest link is usually the part that cannot be copied. Adding machines around a single write desk arrives at the bottleneck sooner.

And surviving a peak is a set of decisions taken in advance, not capacity bought on the day: what gets sacrificed first, what may be made to wait, what is switched off, who is allowed to say stop, and what must already be running before the whistle. Any company can write that list in an hour, and almost none have.
