# AI and ML infrastructure

Two of the four current case studies centre on generative AI, so this chapter
carries more weight than its length suggests.

The data science team has four workstations under desks with GPUs in them.
Models are trained in notebooks, saved to a shared drive, and handed to
engineering as a pickle file and a Slack message. Nobody can say which data
trained the model currently in production, and the person who could has left.
Meanwhile the business has read about generative AI and wants product
descriptions written automatically, with the legal team asking who signs off on
what it writes.

That is two problems, not one. The first is that the ML lifecycle has no
infrastructure under it. The second is that generative features arrive with
governance requirements attached, and the exam tests the governance as hard as
the compute.

Product names and model versions in this area change faster than any book
cycle. Learn the decision shapes below and verify the specific offerings
against current documentation before you sit.

## Vertex AI

Vertex AI is the single platform for the ML lifecycle, and the exam mostly
tests where its managed boundary falls.

**Training** runs your code on managed infrastructure: you supply a container
or a script, Vertex allocates machines and accelerators, runs the job, and
releases them. **Prediction** serves a trained model behind an endpoint that
scales, with a batch mode for scoring large datasets offline. Online endpoints
cost money while idle; batch prediction does not. A requirement to score
millions of rows nightly is batch, and answering it with an always-on endpoint
is a cost error the exam likes.

**Model Garden** is the catalogue of foundation and open models available to
tune or deploy. **Model Registry** versions models and tracks which is
deployed where, which is the direct answer to the pickle-file-on-a-share
problem. **Vertex AI Pipelines** turns the notebook chain into a repeatable
workflow with recorded lineage, and **Feature Store** solves training-serving
skew by serving the same feature values to training and to inference.

Those last three exist for governance questions rather than performance ones.
When a scenario asks how to reproduce a model, prove which data trained it, or
explain why training and production accuracy diverge, it is asking about
lineage, registry, and feature consistency in that order.

**Agent Builder** and the surrounding tooling cover applications where a model
calls tools and consults enterprise data rather than answering from its
weights alone.

## Accelerators

GPUs and TPUs are not interchangeable, and treating them as one line item is a
reliable way to lose a question.

GPUs are the general-purpose answer: broad framework support, custom operations,
inference for many model types, and workloads that mix ML with other computation.
TPUs are Google's own accelerators, built for large-scale training and inference
of neural networks that fit their model of the work, and they win at scale on
supported frameworks. The decision rests on whether the workload's framework
and operations are supported, and whether it is large enough for the
specialisation to pay.

**AI Hypercomputer** is the packaged form of the large-training answer:
accelerators, networking, storage, and scheduling as one system rather than a
pile of instances you assemble. Reach for it when the scenario describes
training at a scale where interconnect between accelerators is the bottleneck.

Availability is a real architectural constraint here, not a footnote.
Accelerator capacity is scarce and regional, so designs that assume you can
create a hundred accelerator VMs on demand in your preferred region are fragile.
Reservations and committed capacity are how you make that guarantee, and
flexibility about region is often the cheaper answer. When a scenario stresses
that training must start on a fixed date, capacity assurance is part of the
correct answer.

## Generative AI patterns

The commonest architecture on the current exam is **retrieval augmented
generation**: rather than fine-tuning a model on company data, you retrieve
relevant company documents at query time and supply them to the model as
context. It keeps answers current because the source of truth stays in your
own store, and it keeps data governance in a system you already control.

Where the vectors live is the design decision. A managed vector search service,
vector support inside an existing database, or a warehouse-native option all
appear, and the sensible tiebreaker is where the data already sits and what
the team already runs. Adding a new datastore to a stack for one feature needs
a justification.

Fine-tuning is the alternative, and the split is worth memorising: retrieval
for knowledge that changes and must be attributable, tuning for behaviour,
tone, and format that the base model does not produce. A requirement that
answers cite sources or reflect data updated hourly is a retrieval requirement.
Fine-tuning cannot deliver either.

**Grounding** ties generated output to a verifiable source, and **evaluation**
measures output quality against a dataset rather than against opinion. Both
appear as answers to "how do we know it is not making things up".

**Human in the loop** is the governance pattern the exam rewards. When a
scenario says a regulated team must approve generated content, or that
customer-facing copy is legally reviewed, the architecture must include a
review step and the correct answer is the one that has it. Fully automatic
publication is wrong even when it is technically superior, and this is a case
where the business constraint beats the engineering.

Inference cost control is the last piece: caching, batching, choosing a
smaller model where it suffices, and setting limits per tenant. The largest
model is rarely the correct answer to a cost-constrained requirement.

## Exam traps

Reaching for custom training when a prebuilt API meets the requirement is the
single most common error. Speech, translation, vision, and document extraction
have managed APIs. If the requirement is a standard task with no unusual data,
train nothing.

Ignoring a stated human-review requirement invalidates an otherwise good
answer. Read the governance sentences as constraints, exactly as you read a
latency figure.

Treating GPU and TPU as interchangeable, or assuming accelerator capacity is
available anywhere on demand.

Fine-tuning offered as the fix for stale or unattributable answers, where
retrieval is the correct pattern.

Deploying an always-on prediction endpoint for work that is inherently batch.

## Recap

- Vertex AI covers the lifecycle; registry, pipelines, and Feature Store exist
  for reproducibility and governance questions, not speed
- Batch prediction for offline scoring, online endpoints only for real-time
  serving that justifies the idle cost
- GPU against TPU follows framework support and scale; accelerator capacity is
  a scheduling constraint that reservations solve
- Retrieval for knowledge that changes and must cite sources, tuning for
  behaviour and format
- A stated human-review requirement is a hard constraint, and the answer
  without it is wrong however elegant
