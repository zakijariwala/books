# Hierarchy and identity

You have an AWS account, or several, held together by an Organization and a
naming convention somebody stopped enforcing in 2022. Permissions attach to
users and roles. Billing rolls up to the payer account.

Google Cloud arranges the same concerns differently, and the difference is
structural rather than cosmetic. Get this chapter wrong and every later chapter
inherits the mistake, which is why it sits second.

## The resource hierarchy

Four levels, each containing the one below.

**Organization** sits at the root, tied to a Cloud Identity or Google Workspace
domain. One per company.

**Folders** group projects. Use them for business units, environments, or
whatever line your governance actually follows. Folders nest.

**Projects** hold resources. A project owns its APIs, its quotas, its billing
link, and its IAM policy.

**Resources** are the virtual machines, buckets, and datasets themselves.

Policy flows down. A role granted at the folder level applies to every project
inside it, and a project cannot revoke what a parent granted. Organization
policy constraints work the same way, so a constraint set at the root binds
everything beneath it.

## Why the project matters more than you expect

An AWS account is a billing and isolation boundary that teams treat as
expensive to create. Google Cloud projects are cheap, and the intended pattern
creates a lot of them.

A project is the unit of:

- **Billing.** Each project links to a billing account. Cost attribution falls
  out of the structure rather than out of a tagging policy nobody follows
- **Quota.** API limits apply per project, so a noisy service cannot exhaust
  another team's quota
- **API enablement.** Services stay off until somebody turns them on, per
  project
- **IAM.** The policy attaches here, and inherits from folders above

Architects arriving from AWS under-create projects. They build one project per
environment and then fight the quota and blast-radius problems that follow. The
exam rewards answers that separate workloads into projects and use folders to
apply policy across them.

> **Exam trap.** A question describing separate teams that must not see each
> other's resources, sharing private network addressing, is asking for Shared
> VPC across multiple projects. It is not asking for subnets in one project.

## IAM, and the habit you need to break

In AWS you attach a policy to a principal. That instinct produces wrong answers
here.

Google Cloud IAM binds three things: a **principal**, a **role**, and a
**resource**. Permissions never attach to a principal on their own. You grant a
role on a resource to a principal, and the policy lives with the resource.

Roles come in three grades:

- **Basic roles** (Owner, Editor, Viewer) predate the rest and span an entire
  project. Treat any answer offering Editor in production as wrong
- **Predefined roles** cover a service and a job, such as
  `roles/storage.objectViewer`. Reach for these first
- **Custom roles** exist for the case where a predefined role grants more than
  the requirement allows. They cost you maintenance every time Google adds a
  permission

Separation of duties appears in section 3 of the exam guide. It usually means
splitting a predefined role across two principals rather than writing a custom
one.

## Service accounts

A service account is an identity for code. It has an email address, it can hold
roles, and other principals can be granted the right to impersonate it.

The decision the exam tests is how a workload authenticates, and the wrong
answers all involve distributing key material.

**Do not create service account keys.** A downloaded JSON key is a long-lived
credential that leaks into repositories, laptops, and CI logs. Google's
guidance treats key creation as the option of last resort, and organization
policy can forbid it outright.

Use instead:

- **Attached service accounts** for workloads on Compute Engine, Cloud Run, or
  Cloud Functions. The workload takes the identity of the resource it runs on
- **Workload Identity Federation** for code outside Google Cloud, including
  CI systems and other clouds. An external identity provider issues a token
  that Google Cloud exchanges for short-lived credentials
- **Impersonation** for humans and automation that need to act as a service
  account without holding its key

to pods. Chapter 12 applies it to Terraform. One rule generates all three
answers: no long-lived credential material anywhere.

## Organization policy

IAM answers who may do a thing. Organization policy answers whether anyone may.

Constraints apply at organization, folder, or project level and restrict
configuration regardless of permission. An Owner cannot create an external IP
address if a constraint forbids it.

The ones worth knowing by name:

- Restrict external IP addresses on virtual machines
- Restrict which regions may host resources, which does compliance work
- Disable service account key creation
- Restrict identities allowed in IAM policies to your own domain

Reach for organization policy when a requirement says "must never" rather than
"only administrators may".

## Cloud Identity and your existing directory

You run Active Directory. Google Cloud needs identities in Cloud Identity or
Workspace, and you have two ways to get them there.

**Federate.** Cloud Identity trusts your identity provider through SAML.
Passwords stay on your side, and your existing joiners and leavers process
keeps working. This is the answer for almost every enterprise.

**Synchronise.** Google Cloud Directory Sync copies users and groups from
Active Directory into Cloud Identity on a schedule. Pair it with federation:
sync provides the accounts, federation handles the authentication.

Grant roles to **groups**, never to individual accounts. Group membership stays
in the directory your identity team already runs, so access changes flow out of
the leaver process rather than out of a ticket to a cloud engineer.

> **Exam trap.** A question about an employee leaving, where access must revoke
> across the estate, is testing whether you granted roles to groups. If roles
> sit on individual accounts, there is no clean answer.

## Recap

- The hierarchy runs organization, folder, project, resource, and policy
  inherits downward
- Projects are cheap, and they bound billing, quota, API enablement, and IAM.
  Create more of them than instinct suggests
- IAM binds a principal to a role on a resource. Nothing attaches to a
  principal alone
- Service account keys are the wrong answer. Attached identities, federation,
  and impersonation cover the real cases
- Federate to your existing directory and grant roles to groups
