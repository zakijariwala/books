# Chapter 2: What Happens When You Upload a Photo

The photo did not arrive.

A guest at a cousin's wedding held up her phone, caught the couple mid-laugh, and tapped share while the applause was still going. The spinner turned. It kept turning. She locked the phone, unlocked it, and the picture sat there in the chat greyed out, halfway sent, with a small exclamation mark beside it. Her sister, three cities away and waiting for exactly this, refreshed and saw nothing.

Then, eleven seconds later, it appeared. No explanation, no apology, no sign that anything had happened. Nobody at the wedding thought about it again.

Nobody except the sister, who spent the eleven seconds wondering whether to ask, and decided not to, because asking would have meant admitting she was waiting.

Eleven seconds is nothing at a wedding and an eternity in the machine. Two hundred milliseconds is a slow response. Eleven seconds is roughly fifty times the budget the entire trip is supposed to take, which means something along the way stopped and started again rather than merely dawdling. In that gap the photo was somewhere, and the somewhere is the subject of this chapter and most of this book. Because that picture did not slide from one screen to another the way a note passes down a row of chairs. It took a longer trip than most of the guests took to reach the venue. It passed through five or six separate businesses, each of which charged for the privilege, each of which could have dropped it on the floor. When the trip works, the whole apparatus is invisible, and the invisibility is the product. Nobody wants to think about freight.

When it stalls for eleven seconds, you get a brief look at the machinery. Almost nobody knows how to read what they are seeing.

So let us follow one photo, all the way, in order. Everything else in this book hangs off this one trip: the warehouse it lands in, the bill it generates, the ways it breaks. The photo comes back in every chapter from here to the end.

## The business problem

Here is why this matters to someone who makes decisions rather than writes software.

You have sat in a meeting where a diagram went up on the screen. Boxes, arrows, labels, a few cylinders. Someone said the write path moves behind a queue and the read replicas take the load, and six people nodded, and you wrote down a term to look up later and never did. The diagram was not hiding anything. It was an honest picture drawn by people with no intention of excluding you.

You had no way to tell which box did what. Which meant you had no way to ask which one was the expensive box, or the fragile one, or the one that breaks first when the company doubles in size. Every question you might have had about money, risk, or timing had to be routed through somebody else, and you could not tell when that somebody else was guessing.

That is the real cost, and it is not embarrassment. Embarrassment passes by lunchtime. The cost is that you defend a six-week estimate to a vice president without knowing whether it reflects real difficulty or a team protecting itself. The cost is that when a customer asks where their data sits, you look at the solutions engineer, and the customer watches you look. The cost is that the bill goes up forty percent and you have no opinion about why, so you have no opinion about whether it was worth it.

None of that requires you to write software. All of it requires you to know what the boxes do.

The trip below is that diagram, in order, with each box named by what it does rather than what it is called. By the end you will be able to trace a real customer action across a real architecture picture, in a real meeting, and ask the two questions that matter at every stop.

## The trip

Think of the photo as a package, not a message.

A message is gone the instant it is read. A package has to be carried, sorted, shelved, labelled, and handed to someone at the far end, and each of those steps happens in a different building, run by different people, billed separately. Your photo is a package. Hold onto that and the rest of this follows.

**The road out.** The tap pushes the file off the phone and onto the network. This is the least glamorous stop and the one that most often explains why something felt slow. The photo is heavy, several megabytes of it, and it has to physically travel. At a wedding, on a venue connection, it is competing with two hundred other phones doing the same thing at the same moment. No amount of clever engineering at the far end fixes a crowded road at this end.

**The front door.** The file arrives at a machine whose only job is to stand at the entrance and decide which clerk inside will handle it. Not a metaphorical door. A specific machine doing a specific job, because there is never just one clerk. On a quiet Tuesday there are a handful. In the ninety seconds after a national final ends and four hundred thousand people upload the same goal, there are a great many, and the front door is the thing that keeps the line moving and decides who goes where.

This is the box people forget exists, and it is the one that explains the most common complaint in software: the app is slow at exactly the moment everyone wants it. That is a queue at the door, and it is a different problem from a slow warehouse, needing a different fix and usually a different team.

**The warehouse.** Now the clerk does two different things with the photo, in two different places, and this split is the part almost nobody pictures.

The first is the photo itself: the heavy part, the actual pixels. It goes onto a shelf in an enormous warehouse. It is written once and, if we are honest about how people behave, read almost never. That wedding photo will be looked at hard for about a day, forwarded a few times in the first week, and then sit untouched for a decade. Nobody deletes it. Keeping it there costs so little that nobody thinks about the decision, which is the only reason the modern habit of keeping everything forever is possible at all. The warehouse is built for things that arrive and rarely leave.

**The day labourer.** The second thing is the small copy. A separate machine wakes up, makes the little preview that shows in the chat, checks it is not something the service refuses to carry, and goes back to sleep. That machine existed, for your photo, for about nine seconds. You paid for nine seconds.

This is the part of the cloud that behaves least like something you own and most like a worker hired by the task. It is also the cleanest illustration of the trade from the last chapter. Nobody bought a machine to make thumbnails. Nobody sized a room for the busiest thumbnail day of the year. The work appeared, capacity appeared to meet it, the work finished, and the capacity went away. That is the whole rent-instead-of-own argument, running four hundred thousand times an hour without anyone thinking about it.

Keep the nine seconds in mind. It is the smallest unit of the trade in this book, and it comes back twice: once when we look at what the machine that did the work amounts to, and again at the end, when nine seconds turns out to be a line on a bill that somebody in finance is asking about. Work that lasts nine seconds and then stops being paid for is either the most efficient thing in the building or a rounding error nobody can explain, and which one depends entirely on how many times an hour it happens.

**The ledger.** Then a line gets written in a book. Not the photo. A line *about* the photo: this image exists, it sits on that shelf, this guest posted it at this second, into this conversation, and these people are allowed to see it.

The ledger is how anything is ever found again. This is worth sitting with, because it is the least intuitive idea in the chapter. The photo on the shelf is mute. It cannot announce itself, it cannot be searched, and the warehouse has no idea what is inside it. If every ledger entry vanished tomorrow, the warehouse would still be full of perfectly intact photographs that no human being could ever locate again. The file is the thing. The ledger is the only reason the thing is findable.

**Why two places at all?** It is reasonable to ask why anyone would split a photograph from the fact of the photograph, given that the split is where the system breaks. Nobody designed it this way for elegance. They designed it this way because the two jobs are opposites.

Holding a large file is a dumb job that has to be done cheaply, at enormous scale, forever. The warehouse never needs to know what is inside the box. It needs to keep several trillion boxes safe, hand one over when asked, and cost almost nothing per box, because nobody is ever going to delete anything.

Answering questions is a clever job that has to be done fast, on small pieces of information. Which photos are in this conversation, who is allowed to see them, which came first. That work needs an index, an ordering, and the ability to answer in a few thousandths of a second. It is expensive per item and worth it, because it happens constantly.

Ask one system to do both and you get something that is bad at each. The industry learned this the expensive way, and the split is the settlement: the heavy, stupid, cheap thing over here; the small, clever, costly thing over there. Nearly every architecture diagram you will ever be shown has this division in it somewhere, usually as a cylinder next to a bucket, and once you can see it you can see it everywhere.

It also has a consequence you will meet again in the chapter on the bill: storing things is cheap and finding things is not. That is why "we will just keep everything" is an easy decision and "we will just export all of it" is not.

**The road back.** Her sister's phone asks the ledger, not the warehouse: anything new in this conversation? The ledger says yes, one image, it lives at that shelf. The phone goes to the shelf and collects the file. The file travels back across the network to a screen three cities away.

The friend sitting at the same wedding saw the photo about half a second sooner than the sister across the country. That gap was not the app being lazy or the sister's phone being old. It was distance. The package had farther to travel, and light through glass is quick but not instant. Physics still charges a toll, even here.

Six stops. One tap. Perhaps two seconds, when nothing goes wrong.

## Which box was the eleven seconds?

Go back to the greyed-out photo, because this is where reading the diagram starts to pay.

The photo stalled, then appeared. Nothing was permanently lost. Given the trip above, only a few things can produce that pattern, and they live in different boxes with different owners.

It could have been the road out, if the venue's connection was saturated by two hundred guests doing the same thing. It could have been the front door, if the queue was long. It could have been the day labourer, if the machine that makes previews was backed up and the chat had no small copy to show yet. Or it could have been the ledger and the warehouse briefly disagreeing: the file had landed, the line had not yet been written everywhere it needed to be, so the sister's phone asked whether anything was new and was told, accurately and unhelpfully, no.

You do not need to know which one it was. You need to know that those are five different boxes, with five different bills, five different failure patterns, and often five different teams. A room that cannot tell them apart says "the app is slow" and assigns the problem to nobody.

And notice what the guest experienced. One thing: a photo that would not send. One symptom, five candidate causes, sitting in five different parts of the system. That distance between what a customer feels and where the problem lives is the reason tracing is worth doing at all. Customers report symptoms. Bills and outages happen in boxes.

## What Meera does with this

Meera runs product at a three-hundred-person software company. She came up through business analysis, she has sat in the second-Tuesday architecture review eleven times, and she has never once asked a question in it.

The next time the diagram goes up, she does something small and mildly rude. She does not nod. She picks one real thing a customer does and says it out loud. "A customer uploads a receipt to an expense report. Walk me through it. Which box takes it first?"

Then she traces it with a finger, box to box, narrating as she goes. The customer taps here, so this box catches it, then it hands off to this one, and this one keeps the file, and this line here records that the file exists and whose report it belongs to.

Two things happen, and both are worth the mild rudeness.

The first is that she finds out how much the room knows. The tracing stops the moment somebody is guessing, and the stop is visible to everyone present. This is not a gotcha, and it should not be run as one. Half the time the engineers discover they disagree with each other about a hand-off nobody had said out loud, and that discovery is worth the entire meeting.

The second is that having named the boxes, she can ask the two questions that matter, at each one. What does this box cost when it is busy? And what does the customer see when this box breaks?

Those two questions are most of this book. They are also, in practice, the two questions an architecture review never reaches on its own, because the people in it are discussing whether the design is correct. Correctness is their job. Cost and failure are hers, and nobody else in the room is going to raise them.

Then there is the estimate. When engineering says the feature takes six weeks, Meera cannot tell whether that number reflects real difficulty or a team building in a margin of safety. She still cannot, and this chapter will not give her that. What she can now ask is which boxes the feature touches. A change that only writes a new kind of line in the ledger is a different animal from one that touches the road, the warehouse, and the labourer as well. She cannot say whether six weeks is right. She can tell whether the estimate covers one box or four, and if the answer is one box she has grounds to ask what the other five weeks are for.

That question does not make her an engineer. It makes her a person the engineers have to answer in specifics, which is a different and more useful thing.

## Inside a real organization

The trip above is tidy because nothing went permanently wrong. In practice, the interesting failures are the ones where the two halves come apart and stay apart.

The photo and the line about the photo are separate things in separate places. That separation is what makes the whole system fast, and it is also the seam along which it splits. The file can land on the shelf while the ledger entry never gets written. When that happens there is no error message, because from every individual box's point of view nothing failed. The warehouse was asked to hold a file and held it. The ledger was never asked to do anything.

What you get instead is quieter and worse: an object nobody can find, sitting in a warehouse, being paid for every month, with no line in any book recording that it exists. It is not lost. It is unfindable, which is the same thing with a longer bill attached. Multiply it by a few years of ordinary operation and most large systems are carrying some quantity of this, paid for and unreachable, discovered only when somebody adds up the warehouse total and cannot account for it.

The reverse happens too, and it is louder. The ledger says the file exists; the warehouse never received it or lost it later. Now the app confidently shows a customer a grey rectangle where their document should be, because the record is authoritative and the record is wrong.

The reason this belongs in the chapter that introduces the trip, rather than in the chapter about failure, is that it is the clearest available proof that the boxes are separate businesses and not one thing called "the cloud." A single system cannot half-work. A supply chain can, and does, and the paperwork gets out of step with the freight in every warehouse that has ever existed.

There is an organizational shape to this that outlasts any particular company. The six stops tend to belong to different people. The road is somebody's network, the front door and the labourer usually sit with the team that owns the application, the warehouse and the ledger often belong to whoever is responsible for data, and at least two of the six are rented from a vendor with a support contract and a phone number. Nobody owns the trip. Everybody owns a stop.

That is why a customer complaint travels so badly through a company. The customer reports a symptom, which is the whole trip. The organization is arranged by box. The report goes to whichever team is nearest, gets investigated inside that box, comes back clean, and is passed sideways, and the honest conclusion of each investigation is that this particular box was fine. Everyone is telling the truth, and the customer is still looking at a grey rectangle.

Knowing the six stops does not fix this. What it gives you is the ability to say, in the meeting where the ticket is being passed around, that the symptom is consistent with three of the stops and not the other three, which turns a search into an investigation. That sentence is worth more to a company than most of the technical knowledge in this book.

## The misconception

Almost everybody who does not work in this field carries the same picture, and it is worth naming because it quietly drives real decisions.

The picture is that the cloud is one place. A grey shape on a slide, up and to the right, with a word written inside it. Something that either works or does not, the way rain either falls or does not. People say "the cloud is down" in the same tone they say "it is raining," as though a condition had settled over the landscape and the only available response was to wait.

It is not one place. It is a supply chain of separate vendors standing in a row, each metering you, each able to fail on its own while the others carry on serving other customers perfectly well. The warehouse can be healthy while the ledger is unreachable, in which case your photo exists and cannot be found. The ledger can be fine while the warehouse is slow, in which case the app swears the photo is there and shows a grey rectangle where it should be. Those are opposite problems. They have opposite fixes, different costs, and often different companies to telephone.

The grey shape on the slide is not a simplification of the six businesses. It is a way of not looking at them.

The clearest test of whether someone holds the weather picture or the supply chain picture is what they do when something breaks. A person who thinks the cloud is one place waits, because weather passes. A person who knows it is six businesses asks which one, because that determines who to telephone, whether the contract covers it, whether the fix is ten minutes or a week, and whether the customers who are affected are all of them or the ones in a particular country. Same outage, two entirely different afternoons.

Once you stop seeing weather and start seeing a supply chain, the questions change shape. You stop asking whether the cloud is reliable, which is a question with no answer, and start asking which link is weakest, which is a question with a name and a contract attached to it. You stop asking why the bill is large and start asking which stop on the trip grew. Neither of those requires a technical education. Both require knowing that there is more than one box.

## Summary

Three things to keep from this chapter.

One tap is a trip, not an event. Six stops, in order: the road out, the front door that picks a clerk, the warehouse that holds the heavy file, the short-lived worker that makes the small copy, the ledger that records that the file exists, and the road back. Every stop is a separate business, with its own bill and its own way of failing.

The file and the record are two different things, kept in two different places. That split is what makes the system fast, and it is the seam where it breaks. Most of the strange failures a customer describes are the two halves disagreeing with each other.

And the cloud is not weather. It is a supply chain. A reader holding that one picture can look at an unfamiliar architecture diagram and start asking where the customer's action enters and what happens to it next, which is the beginning of every useful question in this book.

Every chapter from here is a closer look at one stop on the trip. The warehouse and what it costs to keep things. The worker and what it costs to hire one for nine seconds. The roads, and why distance still charges a toll. The ledger, and the day it tells two people different things. And at the end, the bill with all six stops on it, one line each. The photo comes back every time.

## The Monday question

At the next architecture review, pick one concrete thing a customer does and ask someone to trace it across the diagram out loud, box by box. Then, at whichever box the tracing slows down, ask the two questions: what does this cost when it is busy, and what does the customer see when it breaks?
