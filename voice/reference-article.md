# Reference article: the voice the book should feel like

**how companies make like, a lot of money from your 8h work day**

Companies make a lot of money by keeping you at your desk for eight hours a day. Because when you're exhausted and time-poor, you spend more.

In 1886, workers marched and died at Haymarket Square for the 8h work day. They eventually won: eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for leisure.

Philosopher Nichole Marie Shippen calls this "*unfree time*." The distinction between work and leisure is a capitalist construction. What we call free time is defined entirely in relation to the time we spend producing for someone else.

The problem with industrialisation was that companies needed people with enough time to spend the money they make. Eight hours of work generates wages. Eight hours of sleep generates nothing economically useful. But eight hours of leisure? That is the interval that capitalism needed to colonise.

Leisure as a distinct category (*the holiday, the weekend*) did not exist before industrial capitalism. It was created by disciplined, bounded work time.

Companies feared that workers with too much free time would stop wanting things. The solution was to use marketers and advertisers to redirect the demand for shorter hours into the demand for more goods.

In 1930, John Maynard Keynes made his famous prediction: by 2030, technological advancement would reduce the work week to **fifteen hours**. He severely underestimated capitalism's capacity to convert productivity gains into consuming more things.

The fifteen-hour week never materialised, because the gains were redirected to shareholders and employers. Between 1969 and 1987, the average employed American was working an additional 163 hours per year, the equivalent of an extra month.

This is called the work-and-spend cycle. The workers stay on the treadmill, and as their incomes rise, their consumption rises to match, until the next round of productivity increases offers either more time or more money, and the workers choose more money again.

That's why we spend the most on Saturdays, because weekdays leave almost no time for shopping, dining, or entertainment. The work week compresses desire into two days, and those two days are fully monetised.

David Graeber argued in *Bullshit Jobs* that a significant portion of the modern workforce is engaged in pointless work. The forty-hour week is maintained by an ideology that has made busyness a virtue and presence a proxy for value.

You are not paid for what you produce. You are paid for participating in the performance of productivity. And then you go home and consume things.

Today, artificial intelligence is displacing workers across industries at breakneck speed.

When workers are displaced by machines, they lose their wages. When they lose their wages, they can't afford to buy things anymore. When they can't afford to buy things anymore, the businesses that fired them lose their customers.

The irony is almost too neat. The eight-hour day was invented to turn workers into consumers, while automation was adopted to make businesses cheaper.
