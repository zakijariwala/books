# Chapter 1 (voice pilot): The Great Pizza Box Purge

The servers were called pizza boxes, because that is what they looked like. Flat, black, stacked one on the next, forty to a rack. A room full of them in a business park off the ring road, behind a door that took two cards to open, cooled to the point where the night staff kept a fleece on the back of the chair. When you bought a book at nine in the evening, your order landed on one of those boxes. It had a serial number. Somebody's name was on the lease for the room, and somebody else's phone rang when the cooling failed.

The room cost the same whether anyone bought a book or not. That is the first thing nobody tells you about owning your own computers. You pay for the busiest minute of the year, every minute of the year. On a normal Tuesday the boxes ran at eight percent, warm and bored, drawing power to do very little. The company had bought for the one Saturday in November when the whole country shopped at once, and then paid to keep that Saturday alive for the other three hundred and sixty-four days.

Then sales doubled in a year. Good news, the kind a board celebrates. It was also the moment the room stopped working.

Doubling the boxes meant doubling the room. Doubling the room meant a bigger lease, a heavier power feed, more cooling, and a capital request that went up three levels and came back eighteen months later. Demand had moved in a quarter. Concrete moved in a year and a half. That gap, between how fast customers change their minds and how slowly a building can answer, is the reason the cloud exists. Almost everything else in this book follows from it.

Here is what the vendors will tell you the cloud is about. Innovation. Agility. Digital transformation, a phrase that has never once survived contact with a person who runs infrastructure. Strip the brochure away and the cloud is about the eighteen months. It is renting the room by the hour instead of buying it by the decade, so that when demand doubles you get the space this afternoon, and when demand falls back you stop paying for it tonight.

Renting is not automatically cheaper. A hotel room costs more per night than a mortgage costs per night, and anyone who has expensed a week of hotels knows it. What renting buys is not a lower price. It is the freedom to leave. You are not paying to own the building. You are paying so that a bad November costs you a smaller bill instead of a room you are stuck with for ten years.

So companies ran the purge. They powered down the pizza boxes, cancelled the lease on the room off the ring road, and moved the whole thing onto machines they would never see, in a building they would never enter, owned by a company that rents computers the way a utility sells electricity. The people who did this work talk about it the way people talk about clearing out a parent's garage. Relief, mostly. A little grief. The occasional discovery of something load-bearing that nobody remembered plugging in.

This is where the trouble starts, and where most business readers get quietly lost. The room does not disappear. It moves. Your order still lands on a box with a serial number, in a building with a door, and somebody's phone still rings when the cooling fails. The box now sits in a data center the size of a shopping mall, and the phone that rings belongs to someone you will never meet, employed by a company whose name is on your monthly bill. You did not abolish the room. You rented someone else's, at a scale you could never build, and handed them the pager.

That is the trade this whole book is about. You gave up the room you could touch, and in exchange you got a room that grows on Tuesday and shrinks on Wednesday and never asks you for a capital approval. You also gave up knowing, in any physical sense, where your customers' orders actually go. For most companies that trade is worth making. Meera's company made it before she joined, which is why the architecture review talks about regions and availability zones as if everyone in the room has stood in one, when nobody has.

By the end of this chapter you will know what got purged, what replaced it, and why the replacement sends a bill that makes CFOs cry in a specific and predictable way. You will not know how to configure any of it, and you will never need to. You will know where your photo goes when you upload it, which is the thread we pick up next, and which turns out to be the only question that matters.
